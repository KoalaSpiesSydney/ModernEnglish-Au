Lesson 1 · Introduction

Latin and Greek roots, prefixes and suffixes

The root of a word is the core meaning and in English they come from many old languages. In Academic English they tend to come from Greek and Latin. If you know the root meaning you can understand dozens of words you've never seen or heard before.

Similarly, prefixes (added before a word to change its meaning) and suffixes (added at the end of a word to change its form or grammatical function, e.g. a verb to a noun) usually have Greek or Latin origins.

Most common academic prefixes and many common suffixes are Latin. Examples:

  • Prefixes: pre‑, sub‑, inter‑, trans‑, re‑
  • Suffixes: ‑tion, ‑ive, ‑able, ‑ment

Greek contributes many roots and specialized suffixes, especially in academic fields. Examples:

  • Prefixes: bio‑, psycho‑, chrono‑, tele‑, micro‑
  • Suffixes: ‑ology, ‑graph, ‑phobia, ‑cracy, ‑sis

Recognizing these patterns not only helps you decode meaning but also improves your ability to pronounce and use new words accurately in academic contexts.

    Lesson 3 · Word Parts

    Understanding English vs Memorising Lists

    No one knows every word in their own language. Nobody is an expert at everything. English is huge and always changing, so even confident readers regularly come across new vocabulary.

    What matters is not memorising every term but using knowledge of word origins and context clues to work out meaning as you read.

    By recognising patterns like “beyond,” “back,” “equal,” or “layered,” you can predict meaning with confidence. These word parts also appear across many subjects, so one skill unlocks hundreds of terms.

    Prefixes

    • meta- → beyond, change → metaphor, metamorphosis
    • retro- → back, backward → retrospective, retroactive
    • equa- / equi- → equal, even → equator, equitable
    • strati- → layer → stratified, stratosphere

    Suffixes

    • -ology → study of → biology, geology · always a noun, often an academic subject
    • -fy → make, cause → clarify, magnify · always a verb
    • -ity → state, quality → complexity, activity · always a noun
    • -ous → full of → dangerous, curious · always an adjective

    I move meta when I rhyme — going beyond every form,
    Flip a metaphor, spark a metamorphosis, watch ideas transform.
    I take it retro, stepping back with a retrospective view,
    Even laws turn retroactive when the past comes through. I keep it equa, staying equal, keep the balance in sight,
    From the equator to equitable, everything’s level and right.
    Layers on layers — that’s strati, watch the meaning appear,
    From stratified streets to the stratosphere, the message is clear.

    I study words like ‑ology, that’s the study of the flow,
    From biology to geology, I let the knowledge grow.

    Lesson 3 · Context Clues

    Context Clues: Relative Clauses

    Relative clauses—phrases beginning with who, which, that, where, or when—often define or clarify an unfamiliar word. They act as built‑in explanations.

    How it works

    Find the unfamiliar word, then look for a clause that follows it and describes it.

    Fresh examples

    • “The precipice, which dropped sharply into the valley, made the hikers pause.”
      → The clause explains that a precipice is a steep drop.
    • “She admired his tenacity, which kept him working long after others gave up.”
      → The clause shows that tenacity means persistence.
    • “The artifact,which had been buried for centuries, revealed details about the ancient culture.”
      → The clause shows that an artifact is an old, man‑made object.

    Relative clauses are one of the most reliable context clues in academic reading because they often restate or define the word directly.
