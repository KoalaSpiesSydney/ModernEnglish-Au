The poems of Homer

In 1896, two young British archaeologists, Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt, arrived in Egypt in search of ancient manuscripts. Around 100 miles south of Cairo, near a village then called el-Behnasa, they noticed a series of oddly shaped mounds rising out of the desert. At first the mounds seemed to be a natural feature of the landscape, but on closer inspection Grenfell and Hunt realised they were digging into the ancient rubbish dumps of the ruined Greco-Roman city of Oxyrhynchus. Over the next several years, the two men and their workers unearthed hundreds of thousands of papyrus fragments, discarded by the city's inhabitants nearly two thousand years earlier. Among this vast rubbish heap of forgotten letters, receipts and tax records were pieces of some of the most celebrated poetry ever written, including dozens of fragments of Homer's Iliad and Odyssey.

Exactly when, or by whom, these poems were first composed remains one of the most debated questions in classical scholarship, a puzzle long known simply as "the Homeric question". According to ancient tradition, both epics were the work of a single blind poet named Homer, who lived somewhere along the coast of Asia Minor. Most modern scholars now place the poems' composition in around the eighth century BCE, but many doubt that a single individual, however gifted, could be given sole credit. The poems bear the marks of a much older oral tradition, in which generations of performers memorised and gradually reshaped a shared stock of story and phrase long before anyone thought to write it down.

This oral background helps explain the curious language in which the poems survive. Homeric Greek is not the everyday speech of any historical Greek city or era; it is an artificial, composite dialect, mixing older Aeolic forms with the Ionic Greek spoken along the coast of Asia Minor, and preserving fossilised turns of phrase from centuries earlier still. Repeated formulas and stock epithets, such as "the wine-dark sea" or "swift-footed Achilles", were not simply stylistic flourishes but practical tools, giving an oral performer fixed phrases of the right rhythmic length to fill out a line while he composed the rest from memory.

The Iliad and the Odyssey were never the whole story. In antiquity, they were accompanied by a group of related poems, known collectively as the Epic Cycle, which together narrated the entire saga of the Trojan War, from its causes to the deaths or homecomings of its surviving heroes. Works such as the Cypria, the Aethiopis and the Telegony filled in the events before, during and after the action of Homer's two poems. Yet of this larger cycle, only the Iliad and the Odyssey survive complete. The remaining poems exist only as scattered quotations, brief summaries by later writers, and the occasional papyrus fragment, so that most of what we know about their contents is frustratingly incomplete.

Most Homeric texts that survive from antiquity are written in ink on papyrus, in the form of long rolled scrolls. One notable exception, however, is a curious set of objects known as the Tabulae Iliacae, small tablets of stone or marble, some barely the size of a hand, carved in the Roman period with tiny relief scenes from the Iliad accompanied by inscriptions summarising the plot. Their exact purpose is still debated: some scholars have suggested they served as teaching aids or mnemonic devices, condensing an enormous poem into a portable and easily consulted object, quite unlike anything found among the papyrus fragments of Oxyrhynchus.

Individual manuscripts of Homer have themselves had eventful histories. The single most important surviving copy of the Iliad, a tenth-century manuscript known as the Venetus A, was probably produced in Constantinople before being brought to Italy in the early fifteenth century by the scholar Giovanni Aurispa. It later passed into the library of Cardinal Bessarion, who donated his collection to the city of Venice, where the manuscript was kept, alongside its precious layers of ancient scholarly commentary. In 1797, when Napoleon's forces conquered the Republic of Venice, the Venetus A was seized and carried off to Paris as war booty; it was not until 1815, following Napoleon's final defeat, that the manuscript was finally returned to Venice, where it remains today.

New discoveries continue to reshape our understanding of how these poems were transmitted. In 2025, a papyrus fragment dated to around 285 to 250 BCE, and recovered decades earlier from the rubbish mounds of Oxyrhynchus, went on public display for the first time in New York, having been identified as the earliest surviving fragment of the Odyssey. The fragment preserves three lines from Book 20 that do not appear in the version of the poem read today, physical proof that different versions of Homer's epic were still circulating in the third century BCE, before scholars at the great library of Alexandria set about establishing a single standard text.

A second attempt at domesticating the macadamia

A It took Indigenous Australians tens of thousands of years to build an intimate relationship with the macadamia tree, gathering its rich, oily nuts across the rainforests of what is now northern New South Wales and south-eastern Queensland, and using them for food, medicine and body paint. Yet this long history of use never developed into the kind of systematic crop breeding, carried out over many generations, that produced staples such as wheat or rice. When European settlers eventually took an interest in the nut during the nineteenth century, no local industry existed to make commercial use of it.

The task of turning the macadamia into a global commercial crop instead fell to growers on the other side of the Pacific. Seeds exported from Australia in the 1880s were planted in Hawaii, initially as windbreaks for sugarcane plantations, and by the 1920s a fully fledged macadamia industry had taken off there; within a couple of decades, the nut had become known to much of the world simply as the "Hawaiian nut". Australia itself did not take up macadamia growing as a serious commercial enterprise until the 1960s, nearly a century after the original seeds had left its shores.

Now, a very different kind of "domestication" is under way. Rather than planting more of the same handful of Hawaiian-derived trees, an Australian genomics programme is attempting to systematically map the genetic diversity locked up in the macadamia's remaining wild populations, with the aim of breeding entirely new commercial varieties. Robert Henry, a genomics researcher at the University of Queensland involved in the project, has said that the goal is to build a precise genetic map of the crop that can be used to breed future trees for specific, targeted traits, such as resistance to disease or tolerance of hotter and drier conditions, rather than relying on whichever seedlings happened to be available a century ago.

B Because almost every macadamia tree grown commercially today is descended from a small number of individual trees selected in Hawaii in the early twentieth century, the crop's genetic base has remained remarkably narrow. Recent genome sequencing has traced the maternal ancestry of most Hawaiian-derived cultivars back to wild populations from just two regions of Queensland, Gympie and Gundiah. Henry's research has shown that only a small proportion of the species' available wild genetic diversity was ever incorporated into commercial breeding in this way. A crop descended from so few original trees is left more exposed to disease outbreaks and changing growing conditions, since a mutation that helps a plant resist one new threat may simply not exist anywhere within such a limited gene pool.

C To address this narrow genetic base, researchers working on Australia's National Tree Genomics programme have sequenced the genomes of more than 300 wild and cultivated macadamia trees, drawn from all four recognised species: Macadamia integrifolia and M. tetraphylla, the two species used in commercial cultivation, along with the much rarer M. ternifolia and the critically endangered M. jansenii, of which only around 100 trees remain in the wild. Comparing these genomes has revealed differences between the species in the genes responsible for oil composition and for the cyanogenic compounds that make the raw nuts of some wild macadamias bitter and toxic to eat. It has also identified a single antimicrobial gene, present in all four species, that may offer some natural resistance to the fungal and bacterial infections that periodically damage commercial plantations.

D Breeder Bruce Topp has been using the new genome data to search among wild trees, including specimens of the rare M. jansenii, for genetic traits entirely absent from commercial orchards. Several wild populations, scattered through pockets of remaining rainforest in central and south-eastern Queensland, have never contributed so much as a single seedling to the modern industry, despite carrying genes for disease resistance and drought tolerance that today's narrow gene pool lacks. Early trial trees bred from some of these overlooked wild lines have shown a level of resistance to a serious fungal disease that has previously forced growers to remove infected trees. Topp has been reluctant to identify precisely which wild populations produced the most promising results, noting that competing breeding programmes overseas would benefit from knowing exactly where to look. One researcher involved in the trial described the early results as remarkably encouraging, adding that a handful of the new selections had performed far better than any existing commercial variety under equivalent disease pressure.

E Jolyon Burnett, chief executive of the Australian Macadamia Society, has argued that the improved varieties developed through the breeding programme should give Australian growers a competitive advantage over producers overseas, describing the national programme as one of the industry's most significant research investments and one that will allow growers to build on the genetic diversity found in their own wild trees rather than continuing to rely on cultivars developed decades ago on the far side of the Pacific. Not everyone is convinced that this will be straightforward, however. "Growers have spent sixty years learning to manage a handful of proven Hawaiian-derived varieties," says Dr Elena Marsh, a plant geneticist not involved in the programme. "Persuading an entire industry to plant something genetically new, even if it performs better on paper, tends to happen far more slowly than the science itself."

Genius, autism and the truth behind the myth

Two researchers consider the popular myths and the more complicated reality of autism and exceptional talent

Popular culture has long been fascinated by the idea that autism, and what used to be called Asperger's syndrome, comes hand in hand with hidden genius. Films, television dramas and news stories repeatedly present the same image: a socially awkward but brilliant character who can perform lightning calculations, memorise entire books, or paint a cityscape from memory after a single glimpse. This image has become so familiar that many people now simply assume that most, or even all, autistic people are secretly gifted in some extraordinary way, if only that gift could be uncovered.

There is some truth buried within this popular picture. However, we believe that it seriously misrepresents the reality of autism for the great majority of people who live with it, and also misrepresents the more complicated and much rarer phenomenon of genuinely exceptional skill.

Setting aside the small number of individuals whose abilities are so remarkable that they would be considered extraordinary even among people without any disability, a more accurate picture requires us to separate three things that are often carelessly bundled together: autism itself, the rare condition known as savant syndrome, and general intelligence or "genius." Most autistic people show no unusual savant ability whatsoever, and most people with savant skills are not "geniuses" in any broad sense; their remarkable abilities are typically narrow and specific, existing alongside significant difficulties in other areas.

Consider the case of Kim Peek, the American whose life inspired the character of Raymond Babbitt in the 1988 film Rain Man, and who is still probably the most famous example the public associates with autism and remarkable memory. Peek could reportedly recall the contents of thousands of books and answer complex calendar questions within seconds. Yet later medical assessment suggested that Peek did not in fact have autism at all; his abilities and his social difficulties are now generally attributed to a rare congenital condition affecting the structure of his brain. The man who did more than anyone else to fix the association between autism and hidden genius in the public imagination may never have been autistic himself.

What should we make of this? One conclusion is simply that popular assumptions can be based on famous but unrepresentative cases. There may be a deeper point too. Studies suggest that only around one in ten autistic people show any savant-type ability at all, and that autism accounts for only about half of all documented cases of savant syndrome; the remainder occur in people with brain injuries or other conditions that have nothing to do with autism. Savant skill and autism overlap, but far more loosely than popular belief assumes.

Even when a genuine and remarkable skill is present, its origins are often more mundane than the word "gift" suggests. The autistic artist Stephen Wiltshire is celebrated for his ability to draw detailed, accurate cityscapes from memory after only a brief viewing. Rather than appearing from nowhere, this skill developed over years of near-constant drawing practice that began in early childhood, when Wiltshire, largely unable to communicate verbally at the time, was encouraged by teachers to draw as a way of engaging with the world around him. However striking the results, the explanation has more to do with sustained, repetitive practice than with any mysterious flash of inborn genius.

A similar caution applies to the popular habit of retrospectively diagnosing dead scientists, composers and mathematicians as autistic, based on biographical anecdotes about their supposed social awkwardness or narrow obsessions. Historians of medicine warn that a diagnosis made more than a century after someone's death, from secondhand accounts rather than clinical observation, cannot be verified and risks simply reshaping the evidence to fit a conclusion that has already been decided in advance.

Notions such as "genius" and "hidden gift" are frequently invoked in discussions of autism, but they remain vague and are of doubtful scientific value. Applied indiscriminately, they can obscure the everyday reality of most autistic people's lives, which typically involve no exceptional savant skill at all, alongside real and sometimes significant challenges with communication, sensory processing or independent living. Treating extraordinary ability as the defining feature of autism does a disservice to the great diversity of autistic experience.

A more useful approach comes from cognitive theories that attempt to explain, rather than simply label, the detail-focused thinking style associated with autism. The psychologist Uta Frith proposed what she called weak central coherence, the idea that autistic people process fine detail unusually well but find it harder to integrate that detail into a broader picture. Building on this, the researcher Simon Baron-Cohen has proposed a theory of "hyper-systemizing," suggesting that autistic thinking is especially well suited to recognising and analysing highly patterned, rule-governed systems, from train timetables to musical structure, which could help explain why savant skills so often appear in rule-based domains such as calendar calculation, music or mathematics.

Such theories remain far from settled. Some experimental attempts to replicate their predictions have produced mixed results, and a number of autistic researchers themselves have criticised the underlying assumptions as overly simplistic. Crucially, these theories still do not fully explain why only a small minority of autistic people go on to develop a savant skill at all, while the great majority, who arguably share the same broad cognitive style, do not.

The time seems right to move beyond both halves of the old stereotype: the sentimental myth of the hidden genius waiting to be discovered in every autistic person, and its equally misleading opposite, the assumption that autism is defined purely by deficit. What is needed instead is a more individualised, evidence-based understanding of a condition that, as research increasingly confirms, varies enormously from one person to the next.
