Consumer advice

What to do if your delivery hasn't arrived

Most late parcels sent within Australia turn up within five business days of their expected delivery date. Before contacting Australia Post, it's worth working through a short checklist to see whether the item can be found closer to home.

  • Track your item online — it may simply still be on its way.
  • Check for a card or notification in your letterbox, under your door, or by email or SMS, as this can mean the item is waiting for collection at a Post Office.
  • Ask whether someone else at your home or workplace, or your building's management or mailroom, accepted the item on your behalf.
  • Check whether the item has been left in a safe place, such as under or behind something near your door.
  • Check the address details you gave the sender, in case of a mistake.
  • Look for any known service disruptions affecting deliveries in your area.

If your delivery still hasn't appeared, you can submit a formal enquiry, though how long you need to wait depends on the type of delivery. A domestic item can be queried once it is more than 10 business days overdue. International deliveries follow different timeframes: a Courier or Express item after one business day beyond its estimated delivery time, a Standard item after 10 business days, an Economy or Airmail item only after three months from posting, and a Registered Post item after 25 business days.

To submit an enquiry, personal customers need a free MyPost account and the item's tracking number to use the online missing or lost item form; business customers can log in to a dedicated support portal instead. If your enquiry concerns essential medication, contact Australia Post immediately rather than waiting for these timeframes. Once an enquiry is lodged, Australia Post may contact the sender, the delivery driver, the delivery centre, or, for international items, customs or an overseas mail authority, and will update you until the case is resolved or the item is delivered.

Collecting a parcel

Which collection option is right for you?

A  MyPost Account
This free account lets you track deliveries, manage redirections and holds, and choose a preferred collection point for future parcels. It's required before you can use several other Australia Post services. However, you'll need to verify your identity when you first sign up, and account verification can take a little time.

B  AusPost App
The app lets you track and manage all your parcels from your phone and sends legitimate delivery notifications, helping you avoid scam texts claiming to be from Australia Post. It works on both Android and iPhone. It cannot, however, redirect an item that has already been given to a courier for final delivery.

C  Parcel Lockers
More than 1,500 of these free, secure lockers are available across Australia, accessible at any hour of the day or night. They suit parcels up to 16 kilograms, and a one-time code is sent by SMS or email once your item arrives. Larger items cannot be delivered this way, and you only have two business days to collect before it may be moved elsewhere.

D  Parcel Collect
Available at any Post Office across the country, this service holds a parcel addressed to a nominated branch until you're ready to collect it in person, with photo identification. It suits parcels too large for a locker. Collected items are generally held for about ten days, and the branch's opening hours may not always suit a typical working day.

E  Missed Delivery Service
If nobody is home to sign for a parcel and there's no safe place to leave it, your item is automatically taken to your nearest Post Office or a nearby Parcel Locker at no extra cost. You don't need to arrange anything yourself. However, you won't be able to choose which location it's taken to.

Adapted from Australia Post's official website: pages on MyPost, the AusPost app, Parcel Lockers, Parcel Collect, and missed-delivery redelivery.

Construction Safety

Construction remains one of the most hazardous industries to work in, with many serious accidents caused by falls, being struck by falling objects, or contact with machinery and electrical equipment. A wide range of simple measures can greatly reduce the risk of injury to workers and to visitors on site.

All workers and visitors on a construction site should wear appropriate personal protective equipment, or PPE, to reduce their exposure to hazards. Common PPE includes goggles, helmets, gloves, ear muffs or plugs, boots, and high-visibility vests and suits. Safety signs should also be placed around the site wherever necessary, so that workers and visitors recognise prohibition signs, mandatory signs, warning signs, safe condition signs, and firefighting equipment signs.

New workers should be given a site induction so that they become familiar with how the site operates, and toolbox talks should be held on a daily or more frequent basis to pass on further health and safety instructions. The site itself should be kept tidy, with debris, dust, loose nails and stagnant water cleared away daily to prevent slips and trips. Tools should also be organised and stored properly, with lights and power tools left unplugged, both to prevent damage to equipment and to make it easier for workers to find what they need.

Workers should always use the right equipment for a given task rather than improvising with the wrong tool, since misuse of equipment is a common cause of accidents. An emergency response plan should be in place to direct the workforce during incidents such as fire or hazardous material spills, with a dedicated team responsible for managing any crisis. Physical safeguards such as barriers and fences should be used to isolate people from especially hazardous areas, such as those with high-voltage electricity or toxic chemicals. Tools and equipment should be inspected regularly to check they are free of defects before work begins, and workers should be trained to report any problems or near misses to management as soon as they notice them.

Parental leave in the Australian Public Service

What current entitlements look like, and why they may soon change

Leave for new parents working in the Commonwealth public service is currently set out in a piece of legislation usually referred to simply as the Act. Under the Act, an employee who becomes pregnant is entitled to 52 weeks of leave in total, of which the first 12 weeks are paid. The Act itself does not provide any leave for fathers, partners, or any other type of parent or carer.

To make up for this, individual government agencies commonly provide extra leave, both paid and unpaid, through their own enterprise agreements, so the exact entitlement an employee receives can vary depending on where they work. On average, agencies provide an additional 4.9 weeks of paid leave for birth mothers, bringing their total paid leave to 16.9 weeks, and an additional 3.2 weeks of paid leave for partners.

A recent review of the Act heard from a large number of employees and families about how these entitlements affect their working lives. While many described positive experiences of taking leave, others reported problems caused by leave being unavailable, too short, or subject to strict rules about how it could be taken. The review concluded that current paid leave levels are no longer competitive with those offered by many private sector employers or other parts of the public sector, particularly for partners, and recommended that paid leave be increased if the public service wants to remain an attractive place to work.

Alongside a longer period of paid leave, the review also recommended that leave be made more flexible, for example by allowing it to be taken in single days or at half pay for twice as long, rather than only in one continuous block. It further proposed that, in time, both parents should have equal and independent access to paid leave, instead of one parent's entitlement depending on whether the other parent also qualifies.

The Eureka Stockade

A rebellion of goldminers at Ballarat in 1854 became a defining moment in the development of Australian democracy

A In early 1851 the New South Wales government announced that gold had been found near Bathurst by prospector Edward Hargraves and several companions, and within months the Victorian government reported its own discoveries as well. The belief that ordinary people could dig their own fortune drew migrants from across the country and around the world. Between 1851 and 1860, Victoria's population grew from 76,000 to 540,000, an increase that placed enormous strain on a colonial government with limited finances and a budget already in deficit.

B To raise money, and to slow the flood of new arrivals to the diggings, New South Wales Governor Charles Fitzroy and Victorian Lieutenant-Governor Charles La Trobe imposed a licence fee of 30 shillings a month on every miner, regardless of how much gold they found — at the time, roughly twice the average weekly wage. This was a substantial sum for most miners, and it became a serious grievance once the easily accessible surface gold began to run out in 1852. In that year, the roughly 35,000 miners then on the Victorian goldfields were producing about 5 ounces of gold per head; by 1854, the number of miners had almost tripled while average production per miner had fallen to just 1.5 ounces, making the licence fee feel increasingly unjustified.

C From 1853 miners began holding large public 'monster meetings' to voice their frustration with the licensing system, accusing police of extorting money, accepting bribes and imprisoning people without proper process. Delegations that presented these complaints to Governor La Trobe found him largely unreceptive. Many of the miners were far from politically naive: some had taken part in the Chartist movement for political reform in Britain during the 1830s and 1840s, while others had been involved in the anti-authoritarian revolutions that swept across Europe in 1848. Meanwhile, police regularly carried out licence hunts, searching the goldfields for miners who had not paid their fees, which kept tensions on the diggings high.

D Matters came to a head in the early hours of 7 October 1854, when a Scottish miner named James Scobie was killed in an altercation at the Eureka Hotel in Ballarat. The hotel's proprietor, James Bentley, was accused of the killing, but a court of inquiry quickly exonerated him — in part because one of the officials on the court, police magistrate John D'Ewes, was later found to have taken bribes from Bentley. Many miners sensed a miscarriage of justice. When around 5,000 people gathered on 17 October to discuss the case and demand an appeal, a small group broke away afterwards and set fire to the hotel; those responsible were arrested, further inflaming feeling on the goldfields.

E Over the following weeks, miners elected delegates to represent their concerns, and on 27 November 1854 these delegates approached Victoria's newly arrived Governor, Charles Hotham, to demand the release of the men who had burned down Bentley's hotel. Hotham took offence at being presented with demands, dismissed the miners' grievances, and sent 150 additional soldiers to reinforce the police at Ballarat. Sensing that the authorities had no intention of compromising, the miners held a further meeting at Bakery Hill on 29 November, where a newly made flag was unfurled for the first time: a dark blue banner bearing a white cross and stars representing the Southern Cross constellation, traditionally said to have been sewn by three women on the goldfields. That flag would go on to become a lasting symbol of the Australian labour movement.

F When police announced another licence hunt for the following morning, the miners decided they had had enough. They gathered and marched to Bakery Hill, where an Irish miner named Peter Lalor emerged as their leader. Lalor led the crowd to the Eureka diggings, where men and women present knelt beneath the flag and swore an oath: 'We swear by the Southern Cross to stand truly by each other and fight to defend our rights and liberties.' Using timber taken from nearby mineshafts, the miners then built a rough protective barricade around their position, which became known as the Eureka Stockade.

G Over the next two days, those in and around the stockade carried out military drills in anticipation of a confrontation, prompting Robert Rede, the Commissioner of the Ballarat goldfields, to order an assault at first light on Sunday 3 December 1854. Almost 300 mounted and foot soldiers and police stormed the lightly guarded stockade that morning; the fighting was over within fifteen minutes, leaving at least twenty-two miners and five soldiers dead. Of the 113 miners subsequently arrested, thirteen were put on trial in Melbourne, and all thirteen were acquitted by juries clearly sympathetic to their cause. A Commission of Enquiry that followed recommended abolishing the licence fee in favour of an export duty and a nominal annual miner's right, replacing corrupt officials, and adding new elected seats to the colonial legislature — one of which was won by Peter Lalor himself. Decades later, former High Court judge Doc Evatt would declare that Australian democracy was born at Eureka.
