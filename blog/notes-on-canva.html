Notes on Canva

Share LinkedIn Facebook X WhatsApp Email

Canva is cutting designers, and I’m using Canva more than ever.

With Canva’s recent layoffs in the news, I think it’s timely to reflect on why, and on what I’ve observed in my interactions with Sydney’s design giant. Last year it seemed that Canva was pushing into EdTech, and now it is ‘refounding’.[1]

In fact, just last week, I was invited to a function at Canva, but two days before, I received an email cancelling it, and a few hours before the function was due to begin, I heard the news.

I’ve been watching Canva closely since 2024, when I decided to move into learning design. With a degree in Media Arts, 10 years’ experience teaching and a UX design course behind me, Canva, which was also moving into learning design, looked like a natural fit. Canva was, or has been, a brand, a vibe, a type, from social media template to learning design lead. You could spot the Canva look anywhere.

Now Canva is restructuring, affecting 1,271 people, or 22% of its workforce, and moving staff into smaller teams in Sydney, choosing a future in Australia over Silicon Valley. They describe it as ‘re-founding’ around smaller teams who make bigger decisions.[1][2]

“…getting back to our roots of small, empowered teams with the freedom to make decisions, take ownership and focus on what matters most for our community.”

— Melanie Perkins, note to employees[1]

The way the laid-off staff have been treated has also been heralded as a public relations triumph, with generous packages of a minimum of six months’ pay, even for people recently hired; they keep their MacBook, and Americans keep their healthcare. Perkins even offered US$10,000 grants for start-ups and community projects.[1][3]

In a sense, they are ‘consciously uncoupling’ from Silicon Valley, not just by reducing their staff there, but by giving out an Australian-style redundancy package of respect that differentiates them from the tech bros. Unlike other tech layoffs, they have not claimed AI is replacing their employees; they are simply saying departments are gone to adapt to the new world and survive.[1]

Inevitable?

But was Canva always the precursor to slop? Do you remember those flowery quote posts that populated Instagram 10 years ago, with quotes from Johnny Depp and Buddha, all in that Canva look?

Canva made Adobe skills accessible to everyone. AI has made Canva skills even cheaper and faster.

In May this year, Dan Shipper from Every wrote that AI ‘commoditizes the residue of human expertise’, and that when this happens, what AI can do drops in value, but what AI can’t do increases in value.[4]

I made an infographic to summarise the process he discussed.

Interestingly, CEO Dan Shipper wrote this essay himself, using AI to make it sound like a journalist, and at speed.(I made this image in Claude in about 1 minutes more than it took me to write the information and instructions, I spend about another 20 minutes tweaking in Canva, so it didn't look too AI-boring)[5]

Hence, the value of a Canva template or tool is now virtually nothing — just describe it to Claude.

Why am I using Canva more?

Yet, as a tiny start-up like the giant Every, my output is increasing, and I’m looking forward to hiring and training other teachers as learning designers. If you know the purpose of what you’re doing, you no longer need to filter it through a UX team. If you know your field, you can skip the surveys, get to a prototype quickly, and test and tweak it in the field where you already work.

Why I’m increasingly using Canva is to vary fonts, create unique atmospheric videos and GIFs for my apps to de-AI. I occasionally hand-draw elements, prefer to record real voices, and have an eclectic mix of human and technology elements.

I spend more time removing AI elements than it takes to create them. AI is great at following directions, but it tends to add in text and blocks that create clutter, and it has a look.

And now I have templates generated with AI, edited by a human, I can reuse them, change them and mix them up without AI.

De-AI-ing & designing

We all recall the first time we saw an AI avatar teaching, or a Suno song, or an AI mini drama, and were floored. Now, a year or so later, we are already bored.

In his essay After Automation, Dan Shipper also talks about how abundance creates sameness, and he defines slop as visible sameness.[4] For me, slop is lazy AI and/or AI unedited by humans. To get AI to look good takes a lot of time. With voices, I would say I prefer to just get a human rather than spend time going through word for word to make automation sound natural.

(With voices and avatars, I’m really concerned about the amount of energy used in data centres for something much more easily done in the organic world. Why create them for anything but scammy impersonations?)

However, back to my point. I’m human, and I err off on tangents. Shipper argues that sameness creates demand for difference.[4]

So, I’m not sure what will become of Canva’s retrenched designers: whether they will find themselves working across more projects at once, in sync with experts and AI, trying to create difference, or whether they should find a purpose in their design and become the expert in that particular field.

Nobody knows what Perkins has planned for her refounding.

But I will leave you with another quote from Shipper’s essay:

“Making expert work cheaper does not simply replace experts. It creates more situations where expert judgment is needed.”[4]
