Why Our Teaching Tools Don’t Store Your Files

Share LinkedIn Facebook X WhatsApp Email

Most educational platforms want your materials in their cloud. You upload your lessons, worksheets and assessments, and they keep them for you, usually in return for a subscription.

The tools on Modern English work differently. They run in your browser. You generate what you need, download it, and store it yourself. Nothing is kept on our servers.

Teachers sometimes ask why. This was a deliberate choice, and there are three reasons for it.

1. Your money should go into better teaching tools

Running a cloud platform is expensive. Developers pay for servers, data storage, cybersecurity, backups, compliance and ongoing maintenance. Those costs are passed on to teachers through subscriptions, and they grow every year as more files are stored.

I would rather spend my time and budget on better classroom resources, lesson generators and teaching tools than on storing thousands of files that may never be opened again. Browser-based tools let me keep the core tools free and put development time where it helps learners.

2. Storage has an environmental cost

We tend to think of digital files as weightless. They are not. Every file stored online sits in a physical data centre that uses electricity for computing and cooling, and often water as well.

The scale is growing quickly. According to the International Energy Agency’s Energy and AI report, data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, around 1.5% of global consumption. That demand has grown by about 12% a year since 2017, and the IEA expects it to more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030, slightly more than Japan uses today.

Water is the less visible cost. Researchers at the University of California, Riverside estimated that training a single large AI model in US data centres could directly evaporate around 700,000 litres of fresh water, mostly through cooling.

To be fair, one teacher’s worksheets are a tiny part of this. The point is the habit. Many of us keep years of drafts, duplicates and abandoned lessons in the cloud indefinitely, simply because storage feels free. A simpler approach is better: download the materials you need, keep the ones that work, and delete the ones that don’t.

3. Teachers should control their intellectual property

Teachers create a huge amount of valuable work: lesson plans, explanations, worksheets, assessments and activities. Together they represent years of professional experience.

When everything lives on a third-party platform, you have limited control over how it may be used in future. Most companies are ethical and transparent. However, companies are bought and sold, and policies and terms of service change.

The EdTech sector has a mixed record here. In 2022, Human Rights Watch examined 164 education technology products endorsed by governments during the pandemic. It found that 89% appeared to put children’s privacy and rights at risk, often by sharing data with advertising technology companies.

AI has added a new layer. In 2024, Human Rights Watch found 190 identifiable photos of Australian children in LAION-5B, a large dataset used to train image-generating AI. Some had been taken from school websites and from YouTube videos that were set to unlisted. The families who shared those images never imagined they would become AI training data.

Teaching materials deserve the same scrutiny. Before you upload years of work to any platform, know who controls it, how it may be used, and what happens if the terms change.

What this means in practice

Using browser-based tools does mean taking some responsibility for your own files. A simple system works well:

  • One folder per course or level, with subfolders by unit or topic.
  • Clear file names, for example B1_past-perfect_worksheet_v2.docx.
  • A “tested” folder for materials that have worked well in class.
  • A term-end clean-up to delete drafts and duplicates.
  • A backup you choose, such as a school drive or an external drive.

Your best materials are your professional asset. They should be organised, portable and under your control.

Built for teachers, not for data

The Modern English approach is simple. We build tools that help experienced teachers prepare better lessons faster. We do not collect your work, and we do not need to.
