The Expertise Paradox: Why Good ESL Resources Are Getting Harder to Find

Share LinkedIn Facebook X WhatsApp Email

Have you ever previewed 15 Kahoots before finding one you would trust in class? Have you found one that looked perfect, with a good topic, bright images and the right level, only to reach a question halfway through where the “correct” answer was wrong? Play it, and you teach your students a mistake in front of the whole class.

When I was teaching, this happened more often than I would like to admit, and most English teachers I know have a similar story. It points to a problem that the EdTech industry rarely talks about. Finding teaching materials is no longer difficult. Finding good teaching materials is.

The internet solved the content problem

Twenty years ago, the challenge was supply. Today it is the opposite. By 2018, Kahoot alone reported a library of 51 million public kahoots. Add Wordwall, Quizizz, Teachers Pay Teachers, Pinterest and AI generators, and the supply is effectively endless.

The people creating this material are a global community. English now has around 1.53 billion speakers, and roughly 390 million of them speak it as a first language (Ethnologue, 2025). That means about three in four English speakers learned it as an additional language. This is one of the great success stories of modern communication.

It also means that the people writing English quizzes come from every educational system, every language background, and every level of training, from postgraduate linguists to someone who made a quiz on their lunch break.

What the research says about shared teaching resources

This is not just a feeling. When researchers have looked at popular teacher-shared materials, the results are sobering.

A 2019 Fordham Institute study asked expert reviewers to rate more than 300 of the most downloaded English lessons on three major sharing sites. Most materials were attractive and largely free of obvious errors. However, 64% were rated as either not to be used or probably not worth using. The accompanying assessments were especially weak.

A 2023 study of best-selling history resources on Teachers Pay Teachers found something similar: well designed visually, but 70% of moderate or low quality, and 30% potentially harmful to students.

The most telling detail is that the most downloaded resources were not the best ones. Popularity and quality had separated.

The internet rewards visibility. Teaching rewards knowledge, experience and judgement. These are not the same thing.

The Expertise Paradox

This leads to what I call the Expertise Paradox of EdTech.

The teachers best able to create excellent resources are often the ones who need online resources least. Experienced teachers draw on years of practice, formal training and linguistic knowledge. They can spot a weak distractor at a glance, adapt an activity for their group, or write something better themselves. They are also busy teaching, marking and mentoring, so they publish less.

Less experienced teachers rely more on shared materials, and they often have more reason to share, adapt and publish online. The result is a feedback loop:

  1. New teachers rely on existing internet resources.
  2. New resources are adapted from those same resources.
  3. Errors and weak task design get copied, not corrected.
  4. AI systems are trained on the same repositories.

Over time, internet consensus begins to compete with genuine expertise.

This is not about native speakers

It would be easy to misread this argument, so I want to be clear. The problem is not nationality, and it is not whether the creator is a native speaker.

Research in English language teaching has long criticised native-speakerism, the assumption that native-speaker teachers are automatically better. Non-native English-speaking teachers make up at least 80% of English language educators worldwide. Many of the best teachers I have worked with learned English as an additional language.

When I was checking quizzes for my own classes, the errors fell into two groups. Some were plain carelessness: a native speaker who had not proofread, distractors that did not work, or questions with two possible answers. Others were patterns any experienced English teacher recognises: missing or incorrect articles, awkward collocations, and structures carried over from another language. These are the exact errors we spend our careers helping students overcome. (I collected a few favourites in Kahoot!, Baamboozle and Wordwall’s Funny English.)

The real issue is that expertise has become invisible. To a platform, a carefully built activity by a TESOL-qualified teacher and a rushed quiz by an untrained tutor are simply two quizzes. To the teacher using them, they are not remotely equivalent.

How to check a quiz in 60 seconds

Until platforms make expertise visible, the filtering falls to us. Before using any shared activity, check:

  • The answer key. Read every “correct” answer, not just the questions.
  • The distractors. Wrong options should be plausibly wrong, never accidentally right.
  • Articles and collocations. These are where errors hide most often.
  • The level. Make sure the vocabulary and grammar match your learners.
  • The creator. Look for a profile, a qualification, or a body of consistent work.

Expertise should be visible again

The internet has dramatically increased the supply of educational content. It has not increased the visibility of expertise. That is the gap Modern English is built to close: teaching tools and resources designed by qualified, experienced English teachers, so you spend your time teaching rather than checking someone else’s answer key.
