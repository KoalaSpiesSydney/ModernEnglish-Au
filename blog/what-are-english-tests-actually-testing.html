What Are English Tests Actually Testing?

Over the past few years I’ve been going to every tech and exam seminar passing through Sydney, from Pearson’s PTE Academic to the Duolingo English Test (DET) to CELPIP, one of three tests Australia added to its visa list in August 2025.

What strikes me most about high-stakes immigration exams, and their marriage to technology, is that no one is focussed on the purpose or the accuracy of the test. The companies pitching exams to the Australian immigration department are focussed on security.

I’m going to be drawing on anecdotes from the Duolingo English Test seminars in 2024 and 2025, but I think they point to a general pattern in the exam industry. (As of August 2025, the Duolingo test was not on the list of nine tests Australia accepts for visas, so it is very much still pitching. The accepted tests are IELTS Academic and IELTS General Training, Pearson’s PTE Academic, Cambridge English, TOEFL iBT, OET, LanguageCert Academic, CELPIP General and the Michigan English Test.)

In 2024 Duolingo spoke of ‘equity’: a test which someone in rural India could take at home, on a device as simple as their phone, rather than take the exhausting train ride into crazy old Mumbai. The selling point was that the test was cheaper and more convenient. It takes about an hour and costs around US$70, with no appointment and no test centre.

It has a few innovations in pedagogy that I think need more testing. For instance, the test is adaptive. You’ll be asked some questions early in the test, and your performance then dictates how hard the rest of the test is. If you get the easier questions there is a low ceiling, as these tests are judged on sophistication, not just accuracy.

In 2025 the winds had turned. The free masterclass run by Duolingo and English Australia, ‘Integrity, Authenticity & the Future of Assessment’, held at the Sheraton Grand Sydney Hyde Park on 25 September, was a truly fascinating seminar with little to do with what the test tested and whether it was accurate or useful. Instead, Basim Baig, Duolingo’s Head of Security, facilitated a really interesting session on cheating rings, going into a world which I had glimpses of as an examiner in India: highly organised, technical and determined cheating gangs. The other speakers were Professor Phillip Dawson of Deakin University and Duolingo Assessment Specialist Dr Masha Kostromitina, and one advertised highlight was ‘the economics of cheating — and how to disrupt it’.

The Duolingo test, which was cheap and at home, now requires a second camera on a phone or tablet as well as the computer’s webcam, and a scan of your room instructed by an AI. Duolingo also hires thousands of moderators, not to check the test scores but to watch back video for signs of cheating rings.

For instance, one set of photographs showed the same room with different curtains. The room had been set up to cheat the AI scanner, and the ring thought they could change the curtains and fool the moderators.

My question for you is: what is the purpose of these tests? To outsmart cheaters, or to actually test English that is fit for purpose? Tell me on the contact page.
