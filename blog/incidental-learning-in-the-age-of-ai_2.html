Incidental Learning in the Age of AI

Yesterday I attended English Australia’s Pearson AI workshop, which unofficially happens every year.

Mike Mayor (LinkedIn) came from the UK to Sydney again this year to continue the conversation with Sydney teachers and academic leaders, and to present his data on the international ELICOS industry.

Not the AI apocalypse

Last year the AI workshop was much bigger, which shows how far the ELICOS industry in Australia is shrinking thanks to the cut in student visas.

In 2025 the idea of AI tutors was everywhere, and I expected a doom workshop on how to survive the AI tutor apocalypse. Instead, I was pleasantly surprised. Mayor presented evidence that as AI takes up the slack on the details — the academic assessment and skills students can easily pick up to be future ready — it is the soft skills that computers can’t teach: collaboration, communication, critical thinking, leadership and self-management. And according to his research, that means teachers are needed more than ever.

The Pearson GSE Soft Skills page has more information on the soft skills the Global Scale of English is talking about.

Should teachers police AI?

The workshop began in the same vein, but soon moved on to how to assess students who use AI, and how realistic it is to ban AI for adult students who will find ways around any measures.

Most of the teachers agreed that they should not police AI strictly. That’s not why they became teachers, and the students who really want to learn will monitor their own use.

The main problem, it was suggested, is this: how do you give feedback, and how do you know a student is learning, if they use AI?

AI for the research, students for the thinking?

One suggestion was that students could use AI to source the research and then, with the research at hand, do the critical thinking themselves.

Mayor suggested that the days of students going to libraries and reading through multiple books and chapters to find their information are gone. Now they can use AI to find the evidence and research they want to include in their assignment. And this is efficiency.

But I questioned this. If everybody is using AI to find specific research or sources, they may already have assumptions and biases. They are looking for information that reinforces their bias.

Moreover, if a student spends a lot of time reading, rather than relying on AI to summarise, they get a fuller understanding — and it may occur to them to focus on a new aspect of the topic.

Waiting for the apple to fall

And more, moreover: driving home from the library, stuck in traffic, thinking about dinner, an apple might fall on their head.

I remember the story of the young physics graduate who couldn’t get a university job and was working as a patent clerk. Riding a tram away from the clock tower in Bern, he suddenly had an idea about time. That was Albert Einstein, and the idea became his theory of relativity.

Mayor summed this up as incidental learning.

The group of academic teachers and leaders never managed to come up with a plan, but perhaps apples will fall on their heads about how they can use AI to create future-ready students.
