What's the difference between Academic and General Training Reading?

Both give you 60 minutes for 40 questions across three passages. Academic Reading uses longer, denser texts adapted from books, journals and magazines. General Training starts with shorter, everyday texts — notices, advertisements, workplace documents — in Sections 1 and 2, then moves to one longer, more Academic-style passage in Section 3.

How should I manage my time across three passages?

Aim for roughly 20 minutes per passage, including time to note your answers. If a question is taking too long, mark your best guess and move on — every question carries equal marks, so a hard question in Passage 3 is worth exactly the same as an easy one in Passage 1.

Does IELTS Reading penalise wrong answers?

No. There is no negative marking, so always write an answer for every question, even if you're only guessing.

How do I actually improve my score with practice tests?

Practise under real timed conditions, then review every wrong answer by finding the exact sentence in the passage that supports the correct one. Tracking which question types — matching headings, TFNG, summary completion — cost you the most marks lets you target your revision instead of just repeating full tests.

How the IELTS Reading test works

The IELTS Reading test lasts 60 minutes and has 40 questions spread across three sections. Each correct answer earns one mark, and there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a gap. On the paper-based test there is no extra time to transfer answers to the answer sheet — that has to happen inside the 60 minutes. On the computer-delivered test you type your answers straight into the screen.

Academic or General Training?

Check which one your university, employer or visa requires before you start practising. Academic Reading uses three long passages taken from books, journals, magazines and newspapers, written for a general reader rather than a specialist. General Training Reading begins with short, practical texts — notices, advertisements, timetables, workplace policies and job information — and builds up to one longer passage in Section 3. The general texts are easier to read, so you need more correct answers to reach the same band.

The question types you will meet

Both versions draw on the same set of tasks: multiple choice; True / False / Not Given and Yes / No / Not Given; matching headings, information, features and sentence endings; sentence, summary, note, table and flow-chart completion; labelling a diagram; and short-answer questions. Completion tasks set a word limit, and answers must be spelt correctly to earn the mark. The practice tests on this page mix these types the way the real test does, while the mini drills isolate one type at a time.

How to get the most from these tests

Start with one full test under strict 60-minute timing to find your baseline. Mark it, then go back through every wrong answer and find the exact sentence in the passage that proves the right one. Note which question types cost you the most marks and use the mini drills to work on those. After a week or two of targeted practice, sit a new full test and compare your scores.

Reading Strategy Hacks

Quick, focused strategies for the question types that trip students up most.

Know exactly what each answer means

TRUE means the passage confirms the statement. FALSE means the passage says the opposite. NOT GIVEN means the passage simply never mentions it — even if the statement sounds sensible or is probably true in real life. Don't use outside knowledge, only what's on the page.

Don't chase NOT GIVEN forever

This is where most students lose time. If you've located the right part of the passage, read it carefully, and the information genuinely isn't there, commit to NOT GIVEN and move on rather than re-reading the whole passage again.

Watch the small words

Words like "always", "only", "most", "some" and "never" can flip a statement from TRUE to FALSE on their own. Compare the statement to the passage word by word, not just topic by topic.

Match meaning, not wording

IELTS paraphrases the passage heavily in the statements, so hunting for the exact same words rarely works. Look for synonyms and reworded ideas instead, and remember the answers usually appear in the same order as the passage.

Answer it yourself before reading the options

Read the question stem on its own, underline its keywords, and try to predict the answer in your own words first. This stops the wrong options from planting ideas in your head before you've understood the question.

Scan for synonyms, not exact words

Use your predicted answer and keywords to scan the passage for paraphrased matches, then read that section slowly and carefully — the real difficulty is usually packed into one or two sentences.

Read every option before choosing

Wrong options are often only partly true, or true somewhere else in the passage but not for this question. Check each option fully against the text rather than stopping at the first one that looks right.

Eliminate deliberately

If any part of an option contradicts the passage, the whole option is wrong. Cross out options you can rule out — it narrows your choice and helps you spot exact-word "distractors" the test uses to tempt you toward a wrong answer.

Check the word limit before anything else

Instructions like "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS" are strict — going over the limit makes an otherwise correct answer wrong. Note the limit before you start and check it again for every gap.

Skim the whole summary first

Read the summary all the way through before hunting for individual answers, so you understand its overall shape and can predict what type of information — a date, a place, a process — belongs in each gap.

Let grammar guide you

Look at the words immediately before and after each gap to work out whether you need a noun, verb, adjective or number. If your answer doesn't fit the grammar of the sentence, it's the wrong answer even if the meaning seems close.

Use the passage's exact words

If the task asks for words from the passage, copy them exactly as written — don't paraphrase your own answer in. If you're given a word bank instead, there are always extra words you won't need, so eliminate any that don't fit the grammar or meaning of the gap.
