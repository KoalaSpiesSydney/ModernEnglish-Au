Brett Whiteley (1939–1992)

The Australian painter Brett Whiteley became one of his country’s most celebrated – and most controversial – artists

Brett Whiteley was born in Sydney on 7 April 1939. He was sent as a boarder to a school at Bathurst, in the central west of New South Wales, and later attended school in Sydney itself. From 1956, while still in his teens, he attended evening drawing classes at the National Art School in East Sydney, and joined a sketching group organised by the painter John Santry. At weekends he painted in the countryside around Bathurst and Canberra, producing works such as The Soup Kitchen in 1958. An exhibition of landscapes by the older Australian painter Lloyd Rees, seen at a Sydney gallery, made a lasting impression on him.

In 1959, at the age of twenty, Whiteley won a travelling scholarship funded by the Italian government. The judge who selected him was the established Australian artist Russell Drysdale. Whiteley left Australia in January 1960 and, although he returned many times, he spent much of the following decade abroad.

He settled in London, where he came to the attention of Bryan Robertson, the director of the Whitechapel Gallery, and was included in a group exhibition of recent Australian painting in 1961. His early London canvases were abstract, and owed something to the British painters William Scott and Roger Hilton. When the Tate Gallery bought one of these abstract works, Whiteley became the youngest living artist represented in its collection – a record he still holds. He married Wendy Julius in 1962, and their daughter Arkie was born in London two years later.

By 1963 Whiteley was turning away from abstraction. A painting of the French village where he and his wife had spent their honeymoon is often described as his farewell to abstract art, and from that point the human figure returned to his work. He began a series of paintings of his wife in the bathroom, of which Woman in Bath, painted in 1963, is the best known. He also worked from the animals at London Zoo.

In 1964 Whiteley began a group of paintings based on the crimes of a London murderer. The series shocked many who saw it, and it established a pattern that would follow him for the rest of his career: work that attracted attention as much for its subject as for its handling of paint.

A fellowship took Whiteley to New York in 1967. He lived at the Chelsea Hotel, where his neighbours included well-known musicians, and he was drawn into the movement opposing the war in Vietnam. Out of this came his most ambitious work to date, an enormous painting made up of eighteen wooden panels and running to some twenty-two metres in length, which combined paint, collage and photography. It took him about a year of full-time work. When the completed piece was turned down by the New York gallery he had hoped would show it, Whiteley left the United States for Fiji.

He returned to Australia and settled at Lavender Bay, on the northern shore of Sydney Harbour. The view from the house became his central subject for the rest of his life, and the paintings of the harbour made in the 1970s are the works for which he is most widely known. He continued to paint the inland landscape as well, and produced a well-known self-portrait in 1971 whose title refers to the three bottles of wine he had drunk before painting it.

Recognition followed quickly. In 1976 he won both the Archibald Prize for portraiture and the Sulman Prize; in 1977 he took the Wynne Prize for landscape. In 1978 he won all three in the same year, the only time this has happened. He won the Wynne again in 1984, and in 1991 he was appointed an Officer of the Order of Australia.

Whiteley spent the last four years of his life working in a converted factory in Surry Hills, in inner Sydney. He died on 15 June 1992, at the age of fifty-three, in a motel at Thirroul on the New South Wales coast; the coroner recorded that his death was caused by drugs he had taken himself. His Surry Hills workplace was afterwards preserved, and now operates as a museum devoted to his work.

Regenerative farming: rebuilding the soil

A  Farming has always taken something out of the ground. What is new is the scale of the accounting. Since the spread of farming based on the plough, somewhere between thirty and seventy-five per cent of the world’s soil organic matter – the dark, carbon-rich fraction that holds water, feeds microbes and binds soil into crumbs – is estimated to have been lost. Soil that has lost its organic matter drains badly, blows away in the wind and needs steadily more fertiliser to produce the same crop. The response of conventional agriculture has been to replace what is missing with purchased inputs. The response of a growing number of farmers has been to ask whether the soil itself can be rebuilt.

B  That is the ambition behind what is now called regenerative farming. It is not a single technique but a group of them, used in combination and adjusted to local conditions. Farmers reduce or abandon tillage, so that the soil is broken up as little as possible. Between main crops they plant cover crops, which keep living roots in the ground and stop bare earth from washing away. Livestock are moved frequently between small paddocks in a system known as rotational grazing, so that pasture is grazed hard and then rested. Some farmers spread compost to return organic matter directly. Others practise agroforestry, in which trees are grown among crops or pasture. Across all of these methods, the use of synthetic fertilisers and pesticides is cut back.

C  The word itself, however, is slippery. A review published in 2020 examined 229 journal articles and found that regenerative agriculture had neither a legal or regulatory definition nor a broadly accepted one in ordinary use. Some writers define it by the practices a farmer follows; others define it by the results achieved, regardless of method. Researchers at Wageningen University in the Netherlands, working through a database of 279 articles, found that authors using the same term were applying quite different principles. The four that recurred most often were improving soil health, using resources efficiently, reducing the effects of climate change and protecting water quality. The reviewers’ recommendation was blunt: anyone using the term should say precisely what they mean by it.

D  Some of the claims made for the approach have been very large indeed. The Rodale Institute in the United States, which did much to popularise the term in the 1980s, argued that if regenerative practices were adopted widely they could capture more than the whole of current annual carbon dioxide emissions. Allan Savory, whose method of planned grazing has been influential among livestock farmers, has claimed that grazing managed in this way could return the atmosphere to its pre-industrial carbon level within forty years. Claims of this kind have attracted funding and attention, and they are the version of regenerative farming that most often reaches the public.

E  They have not survived measurement well. A study at the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences in 2016 calculated rates of carbon capture roughly seven times lower than those Savory had claimed. In 2017 the Food and Climate Research Network reviewed the evidence on grazing and carbon and concluded that such claims were unrealistic. A meta-analysis drawing on studies published between 1972 and 2016 found that planned grazing performed no better than continuous grazing when plant cover and plant growth were measured. The pattern is a familiar one in agricultural science: results obtained on a single well-run farm prove difficult to reproduce across many ordinary ones.

F  This does not mean the approach fails. A review of the literature published in 2023 found that regenerative practices can improve soil carbon, soil health or crop yields – but added two qualifications that matter. The improvements appear under particular combinations of climate and soil, and the results cannot be assumed to hold in other regions. A method that rebuilds soil in a wet temperate valley may do very little on a dry inland plain. The honest summary is that the practices work, sometimes, in ways that depend heavily on where they are used.

G  That uncertainty is the main reason adoption has been slower than its advocates expected. Changing a farming system costs money in the years before it pays, and the evidence a farmer would need in order to justify that gap – evidence about profit, not only about soil – remains thin. One agronomist, reviewing a high-profile conversion in the United States, remarked that environmental marketing had got ahead of what farmers could actually deliver. Careful work continues nonetheless. In Australia, the Mulloon Institute, established in 2011, has concentrated on slowing water as it moves through a landscape and holding it in the soil for longer – a narrower goal than saving the climate, and one that can be measured on a single property.

Where did jazz begin?

Jazz is usually said to have been born in New Orleans, but not every researcher accepts that the story is as simple as that

The standard account is easy to summarise. Jazz appeared in New Orleans in the years around 1900, the product of a city unlike any other in the United States. Founded by the French, governed for a time by the Spanish and populated in large part by people of African descent, New Orleans held onto Old World habits after the rest of the country had let them go, and it took a notably relaxed view of music and dancing. Almost every history of the music begins there.

Part of the explanation lies in what the city permitted. From the middle of the eighteenth century, enslaved people were allowed to gather on Sundays at a place known as Congo Square, where African dances and drumming continued in a form that had been suppressed almost everywhere else in North America. These gatherings had ended before the Civil War, but the practices were not forgotten. By the 1880s the Mardi Gras Indians – African-American groups who paraded in elaborate costume – were using drumming and call-and-response singing that clearly descended from West African models. Young musicians who later became famous, among them Louis Armstrong and Jelly Roll Morton, grew up hearing them.

A second element was the city’s Creole population. Creoles of Colour occupied a distinct social position, were often well educated, and included many musicians trained in the European tradition, some of them in France. They were known for their technical skill and their discipline in ensemble playing. Segregation laws passed during the 1890s removed the separate status they had held and reclassified them alongside the rest of the city’s black population. The immediate effect was a loss; the musical effect was that two traditions which had developed apart were pushed together. Formally trained players who read music found themselves working beside players who improvised and who had grown up with the blues.

Brass bands had become enormously popular in New Orleans during the 1880s and 1890s, playing at funerals, parades and dances, and it was from their instrumentation that early jazz took its shape: a front line of cornet, clarinet and trombone, each following its own line at once, producing the dense, overlapping sound characteristic of the city’s music. The cornet player Charles ‘Buddy’ Bolden, active from about 1895, is generally credited with taking the decisive step of improvising on the blues within this format. Those who heard him described what he played as something they had not heard before.

For Adele Brannigan, a musicologist who has studied the city’s musical institutions, this concentration of circumstances is precisely the point. ‘You can find brass bands, ragtime and blues in many American cities in the 1890s,’ she argues. ‘What you cannot find anywhere else is all of them in one place, alongside a surviving African drumming tradition and a body of classically trained musicians who had just been forced into the same bands as everybody else.’ On her account the New Orleans story is not sentimental local pride but an accurate description of an unrepeatable combination.

Theo Marchetti, a cultural historian, is less convinced. He does not deny the city’s importance, but he questions how much of its dominance in the histories is an artefact of the evidence. The first commercially issued jazz recording was made in 1917 by the Original Dixieland Jazz Band – a group of white New Orleans musicians – and it set off a national craze. ‘Once that record sold,’ Marchetti says, ‘everybody wanted to know where this music had come from, and the musicians who could answer were from one city. The question and the answer arrived together.’ Similar music, he suspects, was being played in several places at once, but only one of them acquired a story.

The difficulty facing both positions is that almost nothing survives from the crucial period. No jazz was recorded before 1917, which means the two decades in which the music took shape left no direct sound at all. Researchers work instead from memoirs written long afterwards, from advertisements in newspapers, from the records of dance halls and from union and census documents. Ruth Okafor, an archivist who has worked extensively with the New Orleans material, is cautious about the memoirs in particular. Musicians interviewed in the 1930s and 1940s were describing events of their youth, often in competition with rivals for the credit, and their accounts were shaped by the reputations they had since acquired.

Against Marchetti’s scepticism there is the pattern of what happened next. The musicians who carried the style to Chicago, New York and eventually Europe were, overwhelmingly, from New Orleans. Louis Armstrong’s arrival in Chicago in 1922 transformed the music again, shifting its centre of gravity from the collective front line towards the individual soloist. If the music had genuinely arisen in several cities independently, its spread ought to have looked less like a single line of descent.

Most researchers now settle somewhere between the two positions. The elements from which jazz was assembled were widely distributed across the American South, and no single city invented any of them. What New Orleans appears to have supplied was the particular set of conditions in which those elements combined – and then a generation of musicians who took the result everywhere else. The music that spread across America in the 1920s was shaped by many cities. The music that left New Orleans in 1917 was not.
