Walk the Yorke: Port Clinton to Ardrossan

A guide to one stage of the coastal trail on the east side of Yorke Peninsula, South Australia

A  This stage of the Walk the Yorke trail begins in the small seaside town of Port Clinton, on the traditional lands of the Narungga (Nharangga) people. The full stage covers 27.8 kilometres and takes most walkers around seven hours. If that sounds too much for one day, it can easily be divided into two shorter sections by stopping overnight at the town of Price, which is 7.8 kilometres from the start. From Port Clinton, simply follow the Walk the Yorke signs heading south out of town.

B  The first section brings you to Price, a town that has long been associated with the production of salt. As you approach, you will see the flat, shining salt pans of the Cheetham Salt works, where salt is harvested using the heat of the sun, a method that has been used in the area for generations. Anglers and boat owners will also be interested in Price’s causeway, which leads out to a boat ramp giving access to the waters of the gulf.

C  South of Price, the trail enters Wills Creek Conservation Park, a protected area where the land meets the sea. The walk then continues along what is known as the Samphire Coast, where thick stands of mangroves line the shore. These mangroves provide an important habitat for birds, fish and other marine life, so keep your eyes open and walk quietly if you hope to spot some of the local wildlife.

D  Further along the coast you will reach Tiddy Widdy Beach, a quiet spot with a long, open shoreline. It is here that the Walk the Yorke route meets the CH Smith Trail, and the two paths share the same route for the remainder of the stage. From this point it is a relatively short distance to the end of your journey.

E  The stage finishes in Ardrossan, the largest town on this part of the coast. Visitors with an interest in farming history should not miss the Ardrossan Heritage Museum, where you can see the Stump Jump Plough, a machine invented in the district in 1876 that allowed farmers to cultivate land still full of tree roots. In the warmer months, Ardrossan is also a popular place for catching Blue Swimmer crabs. Walkers looking for somewhere to rest will find a choice of caravan parks, motels, hotels and holiday houses.

Yorke Peninsula School Art Competition

Celebrating the reef on our doorstep

Just off the coast of Yorke Peninsula lies part of the Great Southern Reef, a vast system of rocky reefs and kelp forests that stretches along Australia’s southern coastline. This year, the Great Southern Reef Foundation, working in partnership with the Marine Discovery Centre, is inviting young artists to show what this remarkable underwater world means to them.

The competition is open to both primary and secondary school students. Entries may be submitted by individual students, by whole classes, by student art groups or by schools. Artworks should celebrate the marine life of Yorke Peninsula, and entrants may choose from three themes: local marine species, coastal ecosystems, or reef life and resilience. Any art form is welcome, from painting and drawing to sculpture and collage.

Rather than delivering their artwork in person, entrants are asked to take a photograph of their work and upload it using the online entry form. The closing date for entries is 17 June 2026. A shortlist of finalists will then be selected, and the organisers will get in touch with these entrants during the following week to arrange for their artwork to be collected.

There will be two winners: one from the primary school category and one from the secondary school category. Details of the prizes have not yet been released and will be announced on the Foundation’s website. However, every finalist, not just the winners, will have the satisfaction of seeing their work on public display.

The artworks will form part of the Reef Resilience Photo and Art Showcase, which will open at Ardrossan Town Hall at 6 pm on Friday 3 July and remain open for two weeks. As well as the students’ work, the showcase will feature marine photography and contributions from local scientists, giving visitors the chance to find out more about how the health of the reef is monitored.

How to spot a fake job online

Advice for international students who are new to Australia

Starting life as a student in a new country can be expensive, and many international students look for part-time work to help cover their costs. Unfortunately, criminal networks know this. The Australian Federal Police (AFP) and Australia’s major banks have warned that students, particularly those who are short of money, speak English as a second language and are not yet familiar with Australian law, are being targeted by so-called ‘side-hustle’ job scams.

These scams often begin on social media, gaming websites or messaging apps, where criminals pose as genuine recruiters. They typically promise easy money for simple online tasks, such as liking posts or reviewing products. Some offer a regular ‘salary’, averaging around $1,000 a month, with a commission on top. Once a student has shown interest, they may be asked to hand over personal information such as bank details, a tax file number or a copy of their passport, supposedly as part of the application. In reality, this information can be used to open accounts in the student’s name.

Another common trick is to ask the new ‘employee’ to invest some of their own money first, with the promise that it will be returned along with extra earnings. Further payments are then demanded for things like training, equipment or registration, usually by bank transfer or in cryptocurrency. The promised money never arrives.

The most serious risk, however, is being used as a ‘money mule’. In this type of scam, criminals deposit stolen money into the student’s bank account and then instruct them to transfer it somewhere else, in return for a small commission. Students may not even realise what is happening, but allowing someone else to use your account in this way is a crime. According to the AFP, the consequences for victims can be devastating and may include the cancellation of their visa or even a prison sentence. As one bank representative put it, it is illegal to rent, buy or sell a bank account.

So how can you tell whether a job offer is genuine? Be suspicious of any job that asks you to pay before you are hired, that has a vague description, or that offers a salary that seems unrealistically high. A real employer will usually hold a formal interview before offering you a position. Never agree to let anyone use or ‘borrow’ your bank account, and always check that a company is real by contacting it through official channels. To keep your accounts secure, use strong, unique passwords and turn on multi-factor authentication.

If you think you have been scammed, stop all contact with the scammer and call your bank immediately. You should also report the matter through ReportCyber and Scamwatch, and contact IDCARE if your personal details have been stolen.

Getting around town: how to rent a Lime e-bike

A quick guide for newcomers

Shared e-bikes are a cheap and convenient way to get around many Australian cities, and Lime is one of the best-known operators. Before your first ride, download the Lime app, create an account using a valid phone number or email address, and add a payment method. You will not be able to unlock a bike until this has been done.

To find a bike, open the app and look at the map, which shows the vehicles available near you. If you see one you want, you can tap ‘Reserve’ to hold it for up to ten minutes while you walk to it. When you arrive, unlock the bike by tapping ‘Scan to start’ and scanning the QR code on the handlebars. If the code is damaged or hard to read, try the torch icon in the app, or type in the bike’s plate number instead. Before you set off, it is worth checking the prices shown in the app. You will pay a fixed fee to unlock the bike plus a charge for every minute you ride, and ride time is rounded up to the next full minute.

Next, take a moment to prepare. Release the kickstand, which also activates the motor, and adjust the seat so it is level with your hip bone. Test the brakes by holding the brake levers down while pushing the bike forwards and backwards. Remember that Australian road rules generally require bike riders to wear a helmet, that only one person may ride each vehicle, and that you must never ride after drinking alcohol or taking drugs. You should also study the Ride Zones on the app map, as these may limit where you can go, how fast the bike will travel and where you can leave it.

When you reach your destination, park the bike in a marked parking bay or at a bike rack, or leave it neatly out of the way of pedestrians, and put the kickstand down. Finally, end the ride in the app. Don’t walk away too soon: you will continue to be charged until the app confirms that your ride has finished and the bike is locked.

Designed on Country: a brief history of First Nations fashion in Australia

A  When the ABC broadcast its documentary series The Way We Wore, which explored the history of Australian fashion, First Nations participants shared stories from several periods. They spoke about the clothing that Aboriginal children were forced to wear under assimilation policies during the era of the Stolen Generations, about the part that Adnyamathanha people played in the creation of the famous R.M. Williams boot, and about the rise of First Nations fashion from the 1970s, including appearances at fashion shows in Paris in the 1980s. Yet according to Treena Clark, an Indigenous researcher at the University of Technology Sydney, the series left out much of the background. Missing, she argues, were the handmade garments of the period before colonisation and the involvement of First Nations makers in exhibitions as early as the 1800s.

B  Long before European settlement, clothing and adornment across the continent were shaped by the local climate, by the materials that were available and by cultural stories. Makers worked with animal skins and furs, feathers, human hair, bark, pearl shell and rare seashells. From these they produced footwear, cloaks decorated with designs that expressed the identity of the wearer, and a wide range of finely made jewellery and accessories, including head ornaments, necklaces, belts, bags and caps worn during mourning. Many of these practices are being revived today, and communities value them not only as cultural knowledge but also for the role they can play in healing.

C  From the middle of the 19th century, many First Nations women and girls living on reserves and missions, or attending mission schools, were made to learn sewing and other European crafts. The hats, bags, baskets, jewellery and rugs they produced were often sent to public exhibitions. Baskets from the Coranderrk Reserve in Victoria were one example, while shell-covered baskets made at the La Perouse Mission in Sydney were displayed both in London and at the Sydney Royal Easter Show. At the 1888 Melbourne Exhibition, the Queensland section included pearl jewellery from Thursday Island. How the makers were rewarded for this work varied greatly, and many were never paid at all.

D  By the 1930s, non-Indigenous designers had begun to borrow First Nations imagery, such as boomerangs, shields and hunting figures, for their own products, usually without asking permission. Partly in response, missions and reserves started to set up craft industries of their own. In 1941, a Melbourne newspaper described a bag from Mount Margaret Mission in Western Australia that had been woven from wool using a stitch adapted from traditional Aboriginal work. During the 1940s, distinctive scarves were produced at Ernabella, while artworks created by children at the Carrolup settlement attracted considerable interest from overseas buyers, who saw their potential as designs for printed fabrics.

E  The following decades brought greater economic independence. In the 1950s, the Aboriginal activist and businessman Bill Onus worked with the non-Indigenous artist Paula Kerry to create furnishing fabrics, which were sold through his Melbourne business, Aboriginal Enterprises. By the mid-1960s, women in Coffs Harbour were producing fashion items for tourists in large numbers, and by the end of the decade textiles designed by First Nations people had become a fully developed industry. In 1969, two enterprises that would become well known, Bima Wear and Tiwi Designs, were established on the Tiwi Islands. Fashion parades featuring First Nations textiles were held throughout the 1970s, and in 1987 the designers Bronwyn Bancroft, Euphemia Bostock and Mini Heath presented their garments at the Au Printemps department store in Paris.

F  In the present century, First Nations design has moved further into the mainstream. The designer Robyn Caughlan, together with Benjamin Mach, was the first to bring Indigenous designs to Australian Fashion Week, and in 2023 Denni Francisco’s label Ngali became the first Indigenous brand to present a standalone collection there. Today, First Nations labels show their work in Paris, Milan, London and New York, supported by organisations that help to protect designers from cultural appropriation and to safeguard their intellectual property. For Clark, this long history shows that fashion has always been far more than clothing for First Nations communities: it is a means of cultural expression, identity, healing and social change.
