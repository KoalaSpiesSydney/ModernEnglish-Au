Before you start

Why do we say get?

Get is very common in English. In everyday English, people often say get, not become, receive or arrive.

become tired get tired
receive a message get a message
arrive home get home

Pre-teach: the meanings of get

Get is one of the most common verbs in English, and it has many meanings. The meaning depends on what comes after it. Learn these patterns and most get expressions will make sense.

get + past participle= become / be

get + thing= receive

Mini lesson: one verb, many jobs

Get is one of the ten most common verbs in spoken English. It rarely has a meaning of its own – it borrows its meaning from the words around it. That is why a beginner can use it on day one, and why advanced speakers still find new uses for it.

1 · The main meanings, from A1 to C2

PatternMeaningA1B2 / C2
get + adjectivebecomeget tired, get hungryget carried away, get cold feet
get + nounreceiveget a text, get a presentget the green light, get a raw deal
get + nounbuy, find, fetchget a sandwich, get helpget a handle on something
get + placearrive, reachget home, get to schoolget ahead, get up to speed
get + it / the pointunderstandI don't get it.get the joke, get the wrong end of the stick
get + particlephrasal verbget up, get on the busget over, get away with, get round

2 · Get + adjective or past participle = a change

With an adjective, get shows a change: you were not tired, now you are. I'm tired is a state; I'm getting tired is the change happening now.

With a past participle, get often works like an informal passive, especially for things that happen to us unexpectedly: He got fired sounds more conversational than He was fired. Some combinations are fixed and very common: get dressed, get changed, get ready, get lost, get married, get paid.

3 · Got or gotten?

In Australian and British English, the past participle of get is usually got: Prices have got higher. American English often uses gotten: Prices have gotten higher. Both are correct – just be consistent. For possession, Australians often say have got in conversation: I've got two brothers = I have two brothers.

4 · Phrasal verbs: where does the object go?

This is the rule that causes most mistakes at B2 and C2. There are three patterns:

  • Separable – the object can go in the middle or at the end: She got her point across or She got across her point. With a pronoun, it must go in the middle: She got it across (not got across it).
  • Inseparable – the object always comes after the particle: She got over the shock → She got over it (not got it over).
  • Three-word verbs – the object comes after the last word: He got away with it, I'll get back to you, Let's get down to business.

Some phrasal verbs take no object at all: get up, get by, get ahead, get together. And one verb can follow different patterns with different meanings: get over something (recover – inseparable) but get something over with (finish something unpleasant – the object goes in the middle).

5 · One phrasal verb, many meanings

At C2, the same phrasal verb can mean very different things. Context tells you which meaning is right:

  • get over – recover (get over the flu) · overcome (get over an obstacle) · be amazed (I can't get over how tall you are!)
  • get out – leave (Get out!) · become known (The news got out) · avoid (get out of doing the dishes)
  • get down – lower your body (Get down!) · make sad (The rain gets me down) · start seriously (get down to work)
  • get up – leave your bed · stand · do something naughty (What have you two been getting up to?)

6 · Idioms with get: think of the picture

Many C2 expressions are easier to remember if you picture them literally:

  • get cold feet – your feet stop wanting to walk forward: you lose courage before something big, like a wedding.
  • get the wrong end of the stick – you grab the dirty end: you completely misunderstand.
  • get your wires crossed – like an old telephone line connected to the wrong person: two people misunderstand each other.
  • get the ball rolling – push the ball and the game starts: begin a process.
  • get the upper hand – your hand is on top: you gain an advantage.

7 · When not to use get

Get is perfect in conversation, but in essays, reports and IELTS Writing Task 2 it can sound too informal. Choose a more precise verb:

ConversationFormal writing
get betterimprove
get worsedeteriorate
get (a degree, a result)obtain, achieve
get (an email, help)receive
get to (a place)arrive at, reach
get over (an illness)recover from

Practice

A1 · Which meaning of get?

Read each sentence. What does get mean here: become, receive, arrive, buy, understand…? Say your answer, then check.

  1. I always get tired after football.
    Show answer

    get = become (tired)

  2. Did you get my email?
    Show answer

    get = receive

  3. What time do you get home?
    Show answer

    get = arrive

  4. Can you get some milk from the shop?
    Show answer

    get = buy

  5. Sorry, I don't get this joke.
    Show answer

    get = understand

  6. She got married last year.
    Show answer

    get = become (married)

  7. Get on the bus at the station.
    Show answer

    get on = move into (the bus)

  8. I need to get a haircut.
    Show answer

    get something done = arrange it (a hairdresser cuts your hair)

B2 · Listen and complete

Listen and write the missing get phrase. Then answer the question yourself – it's a great conversation starter.

  1. Have you ever committed a crime? Did you ______?
    Show answer

    get away with it

  2. Have you ever had your heart broken? Did you ______?
    Show answer

    get over it

  3. How much money do you ______ each day?
    Show answer

    get by on

C2 · Listen and complete

Listen and write the missing words. Use the correct form of get – some sentences need got.

  1. The CEO thanked the board for attending and said we should ______ business straight away.
    Show answer

    get down to

  2. Heathcliff despised Linton for bullying him as a child and promised that one day he would ______ him.
    Show answer

    get back at

  3. The investors ______ with the hype and failed to assess the real risks.
    Show answer

    got carried away

  4. The teenagers ______ mischief by having a wild party while their parents were away.
    Show answer

    got up to

  5. She completely ______ and thought she had been fired. Actually, they were offering her a promotion!
    Show answer

    got the wrong end of the stick

  6. The debate ______ when several delegates started shouting over each other.
    Show answer

    got out of hand

  7. I think we ______ – I was told the meeting was on Thursday, not Tuesday.
    Show answer

    got our wires crossed

  8. By the third quarter, the opposition had clearly ______ in the debate.
    Show answer

    got the upper hand

For teachers: lesson notes

Using this page in class

Levels: A1 (40 expressions), B2 (21 expressions) and C2 (65 expressions). Every level uses the same game, so you can switch levels mid-lesson for mixed-ability groups. Each round deals 6 pairs. Add #a1, #b2 or #c2 to the end of the web address to open a level directly – useful for QR codes and homework links.

A 40-minute lesson (any level)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). Write I got a text, got tired, got home and got the joke on the board. Ask what get means each time.
  2. Pre-teach (10 min). Open Pre-teach: meanings of get from the How to play screen. A1 groups focus on the first set of cards; B2 and C2 groups also cover the particles.
  3. Play (10–15 min). Play in teams. Before keeping a pair, a team must say a new sentence using the expression.
  4. Practice (10 min). A1: the meaning sentences, aloud in pairs. B2 and C2: the listening gap-fills, then students ask each other the B2 questions.

Extension ideas

  • Get-free challenge (B2–C2): students retell their weekend without using get – they must find a more precise verb each time.
