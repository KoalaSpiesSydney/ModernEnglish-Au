Before you play: pre-lesson

A 15-minute lesson to teach gradable and extreme adjectives, the adverbs that go with them, and the auction words. Project this section, or print it for the class.

Lesson steps (about 15 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (2 min). Ask: Was it cold this morning, or freezing? Then ask: Can we say “very freezing”? Let students argue before you give the rule.
  2. The rule (5 min). Go through the three types of adjective and the adverbs that go with each, in the first table below.
  3. Pairs (3 min). Students match the gradable adjectives with their extreme partners, then check with the second table.
  4. Really and quite (2 min). Show the two adverbs that behave differently.
  5. Quick check (3 min). Students choose the correct adverb in pairs, then check the answers. Then teach the auction words and start the game.

Two adverbs that are different

  • Really works with both types: really cold and really freezing are both correct.
  • Quite changes its meaning. With a gradable adjective it means fairly: quite good (= fairly good). With an extreme or absolute adjective it means completely: quite impossible (= completely impossible), quite right.

Quick check

Choose the correct adverb.

  1. The film was (very / absolutely) hilarious.
  2. I’m (extremely / absolutely) hungry. When’s lunch?
  3. I’m afraid your answer is (slightly / completely) wrong.
  4. The lecture was (really / very) fascinating.
  5. The results of the study were (highly / utterly) significant.
  6. After the marathon, she was (fairly / utterly) exhausted.
Show the answers

1 absolutely (hilarious is extreme) · 2 extremely (hungry is gradable) · 3 completely (wrong is absolute) · 4 really (it works with both) · 5 highly (significant is gradable) · 6 utterly (exhausted is extreme)

About this game

Absolutely Genius is a free grammar auction for advanced (C1) and IELTS classes on gradable and extreme adjectives. Teams become great families of genius, from the Curies to the Einsteins, and bid on sentences about famous scientists, inventors and artists. Some sentences are forgeries, with an adverb and adjective that don’t go together, such as very unique or absolutely interesting.

Many of the forgeries sound natural in fast speech, so teams have to check the rule, not their ear. At the reveal, the class votes on each sentence, and a family that bought a forgery wins double points if it can correct it and explain why in the redemption round.

Questions teachers ask

What’s the difference between gradable and extreme adjectives?

A gradable adjective can be more or less, so we can say very cold or slightly cold. An extreme adjective already contains the idea of “very”: freezing means very cold, so very freezing is wrong. Use absolutely or utterly instead: absolutely freezing.

Why can’t I say “very unique”?

Unique means “the only one of its kind”, so it has no degrees: something is unique or it isn’t. Use it on its own, or say truly unique for emphasis.

What does “quite” mean?

It depends on the adjective. Quite good means fairly good, but quite impossible and quite right mean completely impossible and completely right.

Why is this useful for IELTS?

Choosing the right intensifier shows precise, natural vocabulary in Speaking and Writing. Phrases like absolutely essential and highly significant sound fluent, while very unique is a common mistake that examiners notice.

How to Play

Advanced · C1 · IELTS & academic English

Every sentence is for sale. Some are forgeries.

Teams become great families of genius and bid on sentences they believe are genuine. A forgery pairs an adverb with the wrong kind of adjective, such as very unique or absolutely interesting.

The rule: gradable adjectives (cold, tired, interesting) go with grading adverbs such as very, extremely, fairly, slightly. Extreme and absolute adjectives (freezing, exhausted, fascinating, unique, essential) go with non-grading adverbs such as absolutely, utterly, completely. Really works with both, and quite changes its meaning.

One person is the auctioneer: the teacher or a student. The auctioneer runs this screen, reads each lot aloud, taps a family's paddle when they bid, and calls "Going once, going twice, sold!"

Read the catalogue first and agree as a team before you raise your paddle. Nobody finds out what they bought until the last lot is sold.

At the reveal, the class votes on each lot before the answer appears. A family that bought a forgery scores double points if it can correct the error in the redemption round.

Genuine lot = 1 point · corrected forgery = 2 points. The family with the most points tops the Rich List.
