Bird flu reaches Antarctica

For most of history, the isolation of Antarctica protected its wildlife from many of the diseases that affect animals elsewhere in the world. That protection came to an end in 2023, when a highly dangerous strain of avian influenza, or bird flu, known as H5N1 arrived from South America, carried by scavenging birds. Since then, the virus has been detected in fifteen species in the region, ranging from flying seabirds such as skuas to penguins. Dr Meagan Dewar, who works with the Scientific Committee on Antarctic Research, belonged to one of the first teams to identify the virus on the continent, and she has since returned to follow its progress.

What has alarmed scientists most is that the virus is no longer confined to birds. It has now been found in the majority of Antarctica’s seal species. According to Dewar, Antarctic fur seals and elephant seals have been heavily affected, crabeater seals and Weddell seals have also died from the virus, and a few leopard seals have tested positive. Researchers now suspect that the seals are not simply catching the disease from infected birds but are passing it on to one another, which would put the mammals of the far south at much greater risk.

The effects on individual animals are distressing to witness. Virus ecologist Dr Michelle Wille explains that the virus attacks the brain, so that infected birds and marine mammals alike display neurological symptoms. Many also have great difficulty breathing before they die.

Yet the outbreak has not developed in the way that experts predicted. When Dewar went back to Antarctica after the first outbreak, she expected to come across hundreds, if not thousands, of dead penguins. This turned out not to be the case. Although that might sound like good news, it raises a worrying possibility: that penguins are carrying the virus without showing any signs of illness. An animal that remains healthy enough to travel long distances while infectious could take the disease to places it has not yet reached. As Wille puts it, the only consistent thing about avian influenza is that it is unpredictable.

To find out where and how the virus is spreading, samples were collected in Antarctica over a period of nine weeks from skuas, penguins and seals. Wille is now testing hundreds of these at a specialist influenza laboratory in Melbourne, Australia, using the same tools and processes that were employed to track COVID-19. The work will take months to complete. Early results suggest the virus is present in the same locations as it was a year earlier, but other research groups have identified cases a very long way from the point where it was first detected.

The clearest evidence of this movement comes from the sub-Antarctic islands of the Indian Ocean. In late 2024, a French team working in the Crozet and Kerguelen archipelagos reported deaths among southern elephant seals on a scale they described as unprecedented and alarming; king penguins, gentoo penguins and brown skuas were also among the victims. Analysis showed that the strain found on Kerguelen had originated in South Georgia, some 7,000 kilometres away. Kerguelen, in turn, lies less than 500 kilometres from Heard Island, a remote Australian territory, and in late 2025, after scientists noticed unusual numbers of dead elephant seals there, tests confirmed that H5N1 had made that jump too.

From Heard Island it is only a relatively short distance to Macquarie Island and the sub-Antarctic islands of New Zealand, and the next step could be the Australian mainland itself. The other likely route is from the north: long-distance migratory birds returning from the northern hemisphere in spring stop along the way in areas where H5N1 is widespread. In Wille’s view, Australia is now effectively surrounded, and the arrival of the virus is a matter of when rather than if.

Australia’s Chief Veterinary Officer, Dr Beth Cookson, agrees that the risk has increased, because so many different bird species can now carry this strain and spread it over long distances. The government has committed more than $100 million to preparations across the agriculture, health and environment sectors. It is also revising its national avian influenza plan after a simulation revealed weaknesses in the way agencies and departments responded. Emergency arrangements have already been tested in practice during outbreaks of a different strain, H7, in 2024 and 2025, which severely disrupted farms producing eggs and poultry. The aim is to detect and contain H5N1 early, but Cookson admits that once the virus is circulating among wild birds, there is little that can be done to limit its spread. Nobody knows how native animals such as koalas and kangaroos would be affected, and there are fears that species which are already endangered could be wiped out.

There are implications for human health as well. By May 2025, 112 human cases had been recorded worldwide since 2020, almost two-thirds of them in the United States during the previous twelve months. The virus does not yet pass easily from person to person, but scientists warn that this could change. The World Health Organization has called for careful monitoring of pigs, since these animals can be infected by both avian and human influenza viruses, allowing the two to mix and produce new strains. In the USA, H5N1 has been found in more than 1,000 dairy herds and is passing from cow to cow; each time it moves between mammals, Wille points out, it has another opportunity to become better at infecting them. Dr Kay Russo, a veterinarian involved in the American response, says that disagreements between government agencies delayed action. She also suspects that some dairy workers who become ill are not reporting it because they are afraid of losing their jobs. The lesson of the outbreak so far, she says, is that influenza rarely behaves as anticipated.

Los Paredones: the Inca at Nazca

A Most travellers who arrive in the town of Nazca, in the desert of southern Peru, have come for one reason: the enormous lines and figures marked out on the ground by the Nasca culture, which flourished in the region from around 200 BC to AD 700. Few of them realise that just two kilometres from the town there is evidence of another remarkable civilisation. Los Paredones – the name simply means ‘the walls’ in Spanish – was built by the Inca, centuries after the Nasca had disappeared, and it attracts only a fraction of the visitors who fly over the famous lines each day.

B The Inca Empire spread outwards from its capital, Cusco, high in the Andes, during the fifteenth century, and eventually took in much of western South America. Los Paredones is usually dated to the reign of Topa Inca Yupanqui, between 1471 and 1493, the period when the empire reached its greatest extent and its territory came to include the plains of Nazca. Some accounts, however, link its construction to his father, Pachacuti, the ruler who began the great expansion. What is not in doubt is the kind of place it was. It was never intended as a town for ordinary people: it was an administrative centre, and the residence of the official who represented Cusco in the region.

C The choice of site was deliberate. Los Paredones stands on the rocky lower slopes of a hill, looking out over the valley, and lies on the route that connected the settlements of the coast with those of the highlands. From this commanding position, Inca officials were able to keep watch over everything that passed between the two regions. Control of transport was central to the way the empire worked, and centres of this kind were set up across the lands it conquered.

D Anyone who has seen photographs of Machu Picchu or Cusco will associate the Inca with huge blocks of stone, cut so precisely that they fit together without mortar. Los Paredones looks very different. Here, only the lowest part of each wall consists of carved stone. Above this base, the builders used rectangular bricks of adobe, or sun-dried mud – the traditional building material of the coast, which the Nasca themselves had used long before for the pyramids at their ceremonial centre, Cahuachi, not far away. The planning, on the other hand, is unmistakably Inca. The large plaza at the heart of the site is trapezoidal, with one end wider than the other, a shape that Inca architects favoured throughout the empire.

E The complex is divided into seven sectors, three of which are better preserved than the rest. A path leads visitors up and around a small hill, and along the way it passes the remains of administrative buildings, warehouses in which goods were kept, and barracks. There is also a tower from which the surrounding area could be observed, and a space set aside for ceremonies.

F Los Paredones offers a glimpse of how the Inca ran their territories. As the empire grew, it imposed its own language and traditions on the peoples it defeated. At the same time, the Inca were practical rulers who took advantage of what was already in place. Their use of adobe is one example. Another may be water: the underground channels known as puquios, which are attributed to the Nasca and can be seen at Cantalloc, a short distance from Los Paredones, were designed to bring groundwater to the surface in one of the driest places on Earth, and some are still used by local farmers today. An Inca settlement in this desert would have had every reason to rely on them.

G Inca rule in the area did not last long: the empire collapsed after the arrival of the Spanish in 1532. In the centuries since, Los Paredones has suffered badly. Adobe does not survive as well as stone, and the walls are crumbling in many places. Around ten per cent of the site has been reconstructed, and paths now separate the areas open to visitors from those reserved for conservation work. Today the site can be reached from Nazca in about five minutes by car, and a single ticket also gives entry to the Cantalloc aqueducts. Even in its present state, it does not take much imagination to picture the centre as it once was.

The fluid future of work

For most of the last century, organisations planned their workforces in a fairly straightforward way. They decided which roles were needed to achieve their goals, identified the skills and experience each one demanded, and laid out career paths along which employees could gradually build up their abilities. According to Mark Marone, a researcher at Harvard Business Impact, that model no longer works. As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes more capable, he argues, jobs are changing in ways that are hard to foresee: the responsibilities attached to a role may split apart, merge with those of other roles, or vanish entirely. He calls this the nonlinear evolution of roles, and in a global study of leadership carried out by his organisation in 2025, preparing for it was identified as one of the three most urgent objectives facing business leaders.

One reason for the change is that AI is no longer simply helping people with individual tasks. Increasingly, it carries out whole processes from start to finish, moving from the position of assistant to that of agent. Marone describes a multinational company that now applies what it calls a ‘4B’ framework to any piece of work, asking whether the human talent required should be bought, built or borrowed, or whether the job should instead be handed to a bot. He also cites the example of the bank UBS, where, since 2024, an AI-driven service has approved loans without any human involvement. The bank’s credit officers have not disappeared. However, instead of making each decision themselves, they now define the limits within which the system operates, test it against different scenarios and coach it.

Those in charge of organisations are affected as much as anyone. In the study, 44 per cent of respondents said their organisation was placing more emphasis on upskilling and reskilling as part of leadership development, and 45 per cent reported growing expectations that leaders would actively support the AI training of their own teams. Marone suggests that leaders are now valued less as decision makers and subject experts, and more as what he terms ‘sense makers’: people who can cope with complexity and guide systems in which humans and AI operate together. Some find themselves with responsibilities that did not exist a year ago, and which may well change again within months.

A further concern is what happens to people at the very start of their careers. Where AI takes over entry-level roles, Marone warns, organisations risk losing the positions in which future leaders have traditionally developed, and this is likely to leave serious gaps in capability later on. There are already signs that this is more than a theoretical problem. The economist Erik Brynjolfsson and two colleagues at Stanford University analysed records from the largest payroll software provider in the United States. In a paper published in 2025, they reported that since generative AI came into widespread use, employment among workers aged 22 to 25 in the occupations most exposed to it, such as software development and customer service, had fallen by 13 per cent in relative terms. Among older, more experienced workers in exactly the same occupations, employment had remained stable or continued to grow. The adjustment was visible in the number of people employed rather than in how much they were paid.

Brynjolfsson and his co-authors offer a possible explanation. AI, they suggest, is effective at replacing codified knowledge – the kind of ‘book learning’ that people acquire through formal education – but is much less able to substitute for the practical know-how that workers build up over years in a job. They also found that the decline in employment was concentrated in occupations where AI tends to be used to automate what people do. Where it is mainly used to augment human work, no such decline was observed.

The labour economist David Autor, of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, takes a more hopeful view. Autor has spent much of his career documenting how computerisation removed large numbers of middle-skill jobs in administration and production. In an essay published in 2024, however, he argues that AI is a different kind of technology. He does not believe that the world is running out of work; for him, the real issue is expertise. Used well, AI could enable workers who lack advanced qualifications to perform tasks which are at present reserved for highly paid professionals such as doctors, lawyers and software engineers, and in this way help to restore the middle of the labour market. He compares AI to a calculator or a chainsaw: a tool which allows people to apply their expertise rather than one which makes that expertise unnecessary. Autor stresses that this is a possibility and not a prediction. AI will not determine how AI is used, he points out; that choice lies with people.

What, then, should organisations do? Marone’s advice to those responsible for learning and development is to stop asking what a role requires now and to ask instead what it is likely to become, and then to work backwards. In practice, this means writing role profiles that are updated as technologies and business models change, building AI into planning tools so that different futures can be simulated, and redesigning development programmes to allow for sideways moves and hybrid roles. It also means making learning available earlier and more widely, not only to those at the top. The aim, he says, is not to predict the future perfectly but to be ready for many possible futures. Fixed job descriptions belong to the past, and people need to be trained to change direction as well as to perform.
