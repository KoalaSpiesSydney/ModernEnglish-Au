Coral Cove Family Resort – Activities for Guests

Bula, and welcome to Coral Cove! Here on Fiji's Coral Coast there is something for every member of the family to enjoy. Here are some of our guests' favourite ways to spend the day.

Children aged 4 to 12 can join the Coral Kids Club, which is open every day from 9 am to 9 pm and is free for resort guests. Our friendly Fijian staff run a full programme of activities, from coconut-leaf weaving and crab hunting on the beach to face painting and movie nights. Babysitting for younger children can be arranged for a small hourly charge, but sitters are in high demand during the school holidays, so please book at the activities desk at least 24 hours in advance.

The resort sits right beside a protected coral reef. Snorkelling equipment in children's and adult sizes can be borrowed from the beach hut at no charge, and every morning at 10 am one of our marine guides leads a free guided snorkel for families. Children under 12 must wear a life jacket in the water. If some of the family would rather stay dry, a glass-bottom boat leaves the jetty twice a day, so you can see the fish and coral without getting wet. Boat trips cost FJ$25 per adult and are free for children.

Every Tuesday and Friday, the chief of a nearby village welcomes resort guests to his community. You will be invited to take part in a traditional kava ceremony, watch a meke (a performance of traditional song and dance) and visit the village school. Visitors should dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered, and sulus (Fijian sarongs) can be borrowed from reception. Please remember to remove hats and sunglasses in the village as a sign of respect.

About 20 minutes' drive from the resort is Sigatoka Sand Dunes National Park, Fiji's first national park. The huge dunes, some of them over 50 metres high, were formed over thousands of years from sand carried down the Sigatoka River. There are marked walking tracks of one and two hours, and the park rangers are happy to explain the history of the dunes, including the ancient pottery that has been found there. The tracks are steep and sandy in places, so strong shoes are recommended. A resort shuttle leaves at 8 am and 2 pm.

If you would like to shop like a local, visit the town market in Sigatoka, which is at its busiest on Saturday mornings. You can buy tropical fruit, handicrafts and freshly caught fish, and there are several cafés nearby serving Fijian and Indian food.

The tropical sun is very strong, even on cloudy days, so please protect yourself, your children and our reef by wearing reef-safe sunscreen on every outing.

Using your New Brewmaster Home Coffee Machine

A Your Brewmaster coffee machine uses boiling water and high-pressure steam, so please follow some basic precautions. Always plug the machine into a properly earthed power socket, and never use it if the power cord or plug is damaged. Do not put the base of the machine in water or try to repair it yourself. Keep your hands away from the coffee outlets and the steam wand while the machine is working, as they can cause serious burns. If you are going away for more than a few days, unplug the machine and empty the water tank.

B Place your machine on a flat, stable surface that will not be damaged by heat or water. Leave at least 10 centimetres of space between the back of the machine and the wall so that air can circulate, and avoid putting it directly under kitchen cupboards, where rising steam may damage the wood. The machine should be close enough to a power socket that you do not need an extension lead, and well out of the reach of young children.

C Before you use your Brewmaster for the first time, remove all packaging and wash the water tank, drip tray and milk jug in warm, soapy water. Fill the tank with fresh cold water and press the hot water button to run two full cycles through the machine without any coffee. Throw this water away. This clears any dust from the pipes and heats the system through, ready for your first cup.

D For the best flavour, use freshly roasted whole beans and store them in an airtight container away from light. Avoid very oily, flavoured or sugar-coated beans, as these can stick to the grinder and block it. Filtered water will give your coffee a cleaner taste and reduce the build-up of scale inside the machine. Never put milk, juice or sparkling water in the water tank.

E To make a café-style latte or cappuccino, half-fill the stainless-steel jug with cold, fresh milk. Before you begin, turn the steam wand on for a few seconds to clear out any water. Then place the tip of the wand just below the surface of the milk and slowly lower the jug as the milk rises. Once you have finished, wipe the wand with a damp cloth immediately, before the milk dries onto it.

F Minerals in tap water gradually form a hard layer of scale inside the machine, which can slow it down and affect the taste of your coffee. When the orange light on the front panel starts flashing, usually every two to three months, it is time to descale. Use only Brewmaster descaling solution, which can be ordered from our website, and follow the steps in your instruction booklet. Using vinegar or other household products may damage the machine and will invalidate your warranty.

G If your coffee is weak, cold or not coming out at all, visit the Help section of our website or download the Brewmaster app, which will guide you through the most common problems step by step. If this does not solve the issue, call our customer service team. Machines under the two-year warranty will be repaired or replaced free of charge.

Our Employee Feedback Process

At Harbourside Health, we believe that the people who do the work often have the best ideas about how to improve it. Twice a year, in March and September, every member of staff is invited to give feedback on their working life through our online feedback form.

The process begins when the People and Culture team sends each employee a personal link to the form by email. The form takes about 15 minutes to complete and must be submitted within ten working days. Staff who do not have regular access to a computer, such as those working on the wards, can fill in a paper copy instead. These are available from team leaders and can be posted in the sealed boxes in each staff room.

All responses are anonymous. Names are not recorded, and results are only reported for groups of five or more people, so that no individual can be identified.

The form has three parts. In the first, staff rate statements such as 'I have the equipment I need to do my job well' on a scale from one to five. The second part asks about workload, training and communication with managers. The final part contains open questions, where employees can describe in their own words what is working well and what could be improved.

When the deadline has passed, an independent research company analyses the responses and produces a summary report for each department. This is usually ready within four weeks. Department managers then hold a team meeting to share the results openly with their staff and to discuss the main issues raised.

Following this meeting, each team agrees on no more than three priorities for the next six months. These are written into an action plan, which is displayed on the staff noticeboard and on the intranet, so that everyone can see what has been promised.

Finally, progress on the action plan is reviewed at the next round of feedback, when staff are asked whether they have noticed any real changes. If you have any questions about the process, please contact the People and Culture team on extension 4400.

Using Claude at Work – a guide for staff and managers

AI assistants such as Claude, which is developed by the company Anthropic, are now widely used in offices to draft emails, summarise long documents, analyse data and brainstorm ideas. Used well, they can save a great deal of time. Used carelessly, they can create risks for both the employee and the organisation.

Key Points

Use an approved account: Staff should only use Claude through the company's business account and never through a personal one. Business accounts are managed by the IT department and offer stronger protection for company information.

Protect sensitive information: Never enter passwords, customers' credit card details or medical records. If you are not sure whether information is suitable to share, ask your manager before you start.

Check the facts: Like all AI tools, Claude can sometimes produce information that sounds convincing but is wrong. Always check figures, names, dates and quotations against a reliable source before you use them in your work.

Give clear instructions: The more background you provide, the more useful the answer is likely to be. Explain who a document is for, how long it should be and what tone it needs. Including an example of what you want often helps too.

Keep a human in charge: Claude can suggest options and point out things you may have missed, but decisions about hiring, staff performance or customers must always be made by a person.

Be open: Let colleagues and clients know when a document has been largely written with AI. Being honest about this builds trust.

Keep learning: Training sessions on using AI tools effectively are held every month. Staff are also encouraged to add instructions that have worked well for them to the team's prompt library, so that others can benefit.

Early Electric Cars: GM's Electrovair and Electrovan

The electric car is often thought of as a twenty-first-century invention, but its history is far longer. Around 1900, electric cars were a common sight in American cities, where they were valued for being quiet and clean. Within two decades, however, cheap petrol and the electric starter motor, which ended the need to crank an engine by hand, had made the petrol car the clear winner. The idea did not disappear completely. In the 1960s, as smog filled the skies over cities such as Los Angeles, General Motors (GM) built two remarkable experimental vehicles, the Electrovair and the Electrovan. Sixty years on, a symposium at the Motor Heritage Museum brought together historians, engineers and analysts to discuss what these forgotten prototypes can teach us today.

The event was well received. 'Most of our visitors are amazed to learn that a major car company was testing electric vehicles in the 1960s,' said museum director Robert Dunn. 'People have written about these cars before, but this is the first time anyone has looked at them in so much detail, from the technology to the business decisions behind them.'

Technology historian Dr Helen Marsh opened the symposium by explaining why GM became interested in electric power at that time. The United States had passed its first Clean Air Act in 1963, and public anger about air pollution was growing. 'Government pressure on air quality gave car makers a strong reason to look at alternatives,' she said. GM presented its experimental vehicles to journalists at a 1966 event called Progress of Power, held at its technical centre in Warren, Michigan. Marsh argued that the industry's enthusiasm for electric cars has come and gone in waves. 'Whenever people become worried about fuel prices or pollution, interest in electric vehicles grows. When those worries fade, so does the interest.'

Battery engineer Samuel Ortiz described the Electrovair II, which was built in 1966 on the body of a Chevrolet Corvair. In place of a petrol engine, it carried 286 silver-zinc battery cells producing 530 volts and weighing around 300 kilograms, which powered a 115-horsepower electric motor. The car could reach 100 kilometres per hour in under 17 seconds and had a top speed of about 130 km/h, performance that was respectable for the time. Its range, however, was only 60 to 130 kilometres, and a full recharge took six to eight hours. 'The motor was never the problem,' Ortiz said. 'The real barrier was the battery. Silver-zinc cells could only be recharged a limited number of times, and in 1967 a single set was estimated to cost about US$15,000, as much as several ordinary family cars. It took another forty years of battery research before electric cars became practical.'

Dr Priya Nair, who researches hydrogen power, turned to the Electrovan, an even more ambitious project led by GM chief engineer Craig Marks. Rather than batteries, the converted GMC Handi-Van used 32 hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells made by Union Carbide, based on technology developed for the space program. The fuel cells generated electricity from hydrogen and oxygen stored in tanks, and the only waste they produced was water. The system was enormous: it needed around 170 metres of pipes, the van weighed more than three tonnes, and only two of its six seats remained. The van could travel an estimated 200 kilometres or more, but it took 30 seconds to reach 100 km/h. For safety reasons it was never driven on public roads, and during testing one of its hydrogen tanks exploded, fortunately without injuring anyone. 'The Electrovan showed that the idea worked,' said Nair, 'but the platinum inside the fuel cells alone cost more than a whole fleet of normal vans. Today, much improved fuel cells are being used to power buses and trucks.'

Industry analyst Tom Becker considered what these experiments mean for the present day. In his view, the obstacles facing electric vehicles have changed less than many people think. 'In 1966 there was nowhere to recharge an Electrovair and nowhere to refuel an Electrovan,' he said. 'Batteries are far better now, but the shortage of charging and refuelling stations is still one of the main reasons drivers hesitate to switch.' Becker added that governments and energy companies, not just car makers, will need to invest if this is to change.

Neither vehicle ever went into production, and both were soon forgotten outside engineering circles. Yet the symposium made it clear that they were far from failures. Dunn announced that the museum plans to open a permanent exhibition on early electric vehicles next year. As Marsh put it in her closing remarks: 'These cars were not dead ends. They were early chapters in a story that is still being written.'
