New Cycle Path: Adelaide City Centre to the North Park Lands

The city council is pleased to announce that a new cycle path linking the heart of the Adelaide CBD with the North Park Lands will open on Saturday 6 March. The 3.2-kilometre route begins at Victoria Square and follows the existing bikeway along Frome Street before crossing North Terrace at a new signal-controlled crossing. From there it continues along Frome Road, past the university and the entrance to the zoo, and crosses the River Torrens on a widened section of the Albert Bridge.

North of the river, the path runs along the eastern edge of the North Adelaide Golf Course before joining the Adelaide Park Lands Trail, the 19.5-kilometre loop that circles the city. This means that riders can now travel from the city centre to any part of the Park Lands without having to share a lane with cars.

On the older Frome Street bikeway, cyclists were separated from traffic only by a row of flexible plastic posts. On the new sections, a raised concrete kerb has been built instead. The path is lit along its whole length, and three bike repair stations, each equipped with a pump and a set of basic tools, have been installed at Victoria Square, the Albert Bridge and the golf course car park. Covered racks for up to 40 bicycles are available beside the golf course clubhouse, where the kiosk now sells takeaway coffee.

Please remember that the golf course is a working sporting facility. The path is fenced where it passes the ninth and tenth holes, and cyclists must not leave the path to cross the fairways. Two marked crossing points are provided for the use of walkers and golfers only. While redevelopment work on the course continues this year, the section of path beside the practice area may be closed at short notice. When this happens, signs will direct riders to a temporary route along War Memorial Drive.

Once it enters the Park Lands, the path is shared with walkers and runners, and a speed limit of 15 km/h applies. Electric bikes are welcome on the whole route, but e-scooters are only permitted on the city section south of the river. Dogs must be kept on a lead at all times.

The project has been jointly funded by the council and the state government. A free guided ride will leave Victoria Square at 9 am on the opening day. No booking is required, but children under 12 must be accompanied by an adult. Comments on the new path can be left on the council's website until the end of April.

How to Make Your Workplace Healthier

Most of us spend more of our waking hours at work than anywhere else, so small changes to the way we work can make a big difference to our health. Here are some simple ideas that employers and staff can put into practice straight away.

Get the set-up right

Many aches and pains start with a badly arranged desk. Your chair should support your lower back, and your feet should rest flat on the floor. Place your computer screen about an arm's length away, with the top of it level with your eyes, so that you are not bending your neck forward all day.

Keep moving

Sitting still for long periods slows the body down. Try to stand up and move around at least every 30 minutes, even if it is only to fetch a glass of water. If a meeting involves just two or three people, consider making it a walking meeting outside: fresh air and a change of scene often lead to better ideas.

Rest your eyes

Staring at a screen for hours can leave your eyes tired and dry. Follow the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something about six metres away for 20 seconds.

Eat and drink well

Keep a bottle of water on your desk and refill it through the day. Managers can help too: replacing the office biscuit tin with a bowl of fruit is a cheap and popular change. And however busy you are, take your lunch break away from your desk.

Look after each other

Coming to work with a cold or flu may seem dedicated, but it usually means that others become ill too. Staff who feel unwell should rest or, if they are well enough, work from home. Finally, regular cleaning of shared keyboards, phones and kitchen surfaces helps to stop germs from spreading.

This text is original writing for practice-test purposes, based on widely published workplace health advice about desk set-up, regular movement, eye strain (the 20-20-20 rule), healthy eating and staying home when unwell.

How to Pass an AI Interview

More and more employers now use artificial intelligence (AI) to screen job applicants. If you are invited to an AI interview, the following advice will help you perform at your best.

What is an AI interview?

In most cases it is a one-way interview: there is no live interviewer. Questions appear on your screen, and you record your answers using the camera on your computer or phone. You usually have around 30 seconds to think before recording begins and two to three minutes to answer. The software then analyses what you say, together with your voice and facial expressions. Later, a recruiter may watch your recording, so remember that a real person could see it.

Before the interview

Test your camera and microphone well in advance, and choose a quiet room where you will not be interrupted. Sit with a light in front of you rather than behind you, and make sure the background is plain and tidy. Most platforms offer a practice question; use it to get used to the timing. Finally, read the job advertisement carefully and note down its keywords, since the software may be looking for these in your answers.

During the interview

Look directly into the camera lens rather than at your own image on the screen. It may help to imagine that a real person is sitting behind the camera: this will make you sound more natural. Smile, show enthusiasm and use the same gestures you would use in a face-to-face conversation. Answer every question honestly, as the AI may be designed to notice answers that are exaggerated or untrue. Avoid reading from notes: your eyes will keep moving away from the camera, and this can suggest that you lack confidence.

Sources▾
This text is original writing for practice-test purposes, drawing on general advice about one-way video interviews, including Thea Kelley's article “How to Pass an AI Interview” (jobsearchandinterviewcoach.com).

Do Pets Know They've Done Something Wrong?

A Every dog owner knows the scene. You arrive home to find a cushion torn to pieces or the rubbish bin knocked over, and your dog greets you with its head low and its eyes turned away. It is hard not to conclude that the animal knows exactly what it has done and feels sorry about it. Indeed, in surveys, a large majority of dog owners say they believe their pets are capable of feeling guilt. But is that really what the famous 'guilty look' tells us?

B Animal behaviour experts describe the look in some detail. The dog lowers its head, avoids eye contact and hunches its shoulders. It may tuck its tail between its legs or thump it slowly on the floor while backing away or rolling over. To us, these signals look remarkably like human shame. However, the same postures are used by dogs and wolves in their dealings with one another. A lower-ranking animal will adopt them when faced with a stronger or angrier member of the group, to show that it poses no threat and to prevent a fight. Scientists call this appeasement behaviour.

C In 2009, the psychologist Alexandra Horowitz of Barnard College in New York designed an experiment to find out what really triggers the look. Each owner told their dog not to eat a treat and then left the room. While they were away, the researcher either allowed the dog to eat the treat or removed it. When the owners returned, however, they were not always told the truth: some were told that their dog had eaten the treat when in fact it had not, and others the reverse. Owners who believed their dog had disobeyed scolded it. The results were striking. Whether a dog looked guilty had little to do with whether it had actually eaten the treat. The look appeared most strongly when dogs were scolded, and it was most intense of all in innocent dogs that were told off.

D Three years later, a team led by Julie Hecht in Budapest approached the question from a different angle. Dogs were left alone in a room with a piece of food they had been forbidden to take, and their owners were then asked to judge, simply from the way their dog greeted them, whether it had eaten the food. The owners did no better than they would have done by guessing. What did predict a guilty-looking greeting was the dog's history: animals that had been scolded in the past for similar behaviour tended to act guilty whether they had misbehaved or not.

E Taken together, these studies suggest that the guilty look is not a sign of a bad conscience but a response to the owner. Dogs are extremely sensitive to our tone of voice, facial expressions and body language. When they sense that we are frustrated or angry, they respond with calming signals in the hope of avoiding trouble. Some dogs may also learn that the combination of their owner and a mess on the floor means an unpleasant few minutes, even if someone else, such as the family cat, caused the mess.

F None of this means that animals have no sense of social rules. In a well-known study at the University of Vienna, Friederike Range and her colleagues asked pairs of dogs to give a paw. When one dog received a food reward for doing so and its partner did not, the unrewarded dog soon refused to cooperate, whereas dogs tested on their own continued for much longer without any reward. The American biologist Marc Bekoff has also observed that dogs follow clear rules when they play: a dog that bites too hard or plays too roughly is often avoided by the others. Behaviour like this points to a basic awareness of fairness, even if it falls short of guilt as humans experience it.

G Understanding what the look really means has practical consequences. Many owners punish their pets when they discover damage, sometimes hours after it happened. Yet a dog or cat cannot connect a telling-off with actions that took place long before. All the animal learns is that its owner sometimes becomes angry for no obvious reason, and it may become afraid of the person it depends on. Over time, this can harm the relationship between pet and owner and even lead to new behaviour problems.

H Veterinary behaviourists recommend a different approach. Rather than punishing mistakes after the event, owners should prevent them from happening: keep tempting items out of reach, supervise young animals closely, and interrupt unwanted behaviour calmly the moment it starts. Most importantly, they should reward the behaviour they do want to see, with praise, play or a small treat. A pet that is taught what to do, rather than punished for what it has done, is likely to be a happier and better-behaved companion.

Sources▾
This passage is original writing for practice-test purposes, drawing on VCA Animal Hospitals' article “Do Pets Know They've Done Something Wrong?” and on published research by Alexandra Horowitz (“Disambiguating the ‘guilty look’”, Behavioural Processes, 2009), Julie Hecht and colleagues (2012), Friederike Range and colleagues (inequity aversion in dogs, 2008) and Marc Bekoff's work on fairness in animal play. The studies are summarised in simplified form.
