Literal & Straightforward Phrasal Verbs

These phrasal verbs mean exactly what their parts suggest — physical, directional, and concrete. They are the foundation of everyday English conversation.

Key Idea

In a literal phrasal verb, the preposition describes a physical direction or position. "Stand up" — the preposition up means physically upward. Easy!

Lesson 1 · Word Order

Word Order & Pronoun Position

Most literal phrasal verbs are separable — but with pronouns the rule is strict. Flip the cards to learn the rules, then study the full table.

The Golden Rule of Pronouns

When the object is a pronoun (it, them, him, her, us, me), it ALWAYS goes BETWEEN the verb and the preposition. You cannot put it after the preposition.

✓ Correct: "Turn it off."  |  ✗ Wrong: "Turn off it."

Lesson 4 · Word Order

Word Order in Formal English

Formal and professional phrasal verbs follow the same grammar rules — but in formal written English there is a strong stylistic preference to keep them together.

Style Note for Academic & Business Writing

In formal prose, keep the phrasal verb unseparated even when separation is grammatically permitted.

Preferred: "The study carried out a controlled experiment."

Also correct: "The study carried a controlled experiment out." — but less natural in formal prose.

Lesson 5 · Pronunciation

How Phrasal Verbs Really Sound

Written English and spoken English are very different. In natural speech, phrasal verbs are compressed, linked, and weakened in ways that can make them almost unrecognisable to learners. This lesson teaches you to both understand and produce them naturally.

Chunking

Phrasal verbs are spoken as a single phonetic unit — a "chunk". The verb and preposition blend together without a pause between them.

pick UP → "pickUP"
/pɪˈkʌp/ — one smooth unit

Stress Pattern

In a phrasal verb, the stress almost always falls on the preposition, not the verb. This is the opposite of many learners' instinct.

turn OFF, give UP, break DOWN
stress = CAPS — the preposition carries it

The Schwa (ə)

In connected speech, prepositions like to, of, for, at, from are reduced to a weak schwa sound /ə/ — not their full vowel.

"out of" → "outtə"
/ˈaʊtə/ — the "of" disappears

Linking

When a phrasal verb ends in a consonant and the next word begins with a vowel, the sounds link together — there is no gap.

"pick it up" → "pickitup"
/ˈpɪkɪtʌp/ — three words, one sound
Why This Matters

If you stress the verb instead of the preposition, native speakers may not understand you, or you may sound unnatural. If you don't reduce prepositions to their weak forms, your speech will sound "textbook" — grammatically correct but not fluent.

Lesson 5 · Chunking

Chunking — One Unit, One Breath

Native speakers do not pause between the verb and preposition. They say the whole phrasal verb as one unbroken phonetic chunk — like a single long word. The preposition is stressed and slightly louder than the verb.

The Rule

Verb = unstressed, short, quick.  |  Preposition = stressed, longer, louder.

Think of it like a two-syllable word where the second syllable is stressed: to-DAY, be-FORE, a-GAIN — same pattern: pick-UP, sit-DOWN, wake-UP.

Lesson 5 · Schwa & Weak Forms

The Schwa & Weak Preposition Forms

The schwa /ə/ is the most common sound in English. In connected speech, prepositions and prepositions are regularly reduced to a schwa — their "full" pronunciation disappears and is replaced by a brief, unstressed neutral vowel.

What is the schwa?

The schwa /ə/ sounds like a very short, relaxed "uh". It is the sound in the unstressed syllables of words like about, alone, system, common. In rapid speech, many short function words reduce to it.

to → /tə/ of → /əv/ or /ə/ for → /fə/ at → /ət/ from → /frəm/ and → /ən/

Listening Tip

When you can't hear a preposition clearly in natural speech, it has almost certainly been reduced to a schwa. Train your ear by watching Australian or British TV without subtitles and listening for the "uh" sound between phrasal verb components.
