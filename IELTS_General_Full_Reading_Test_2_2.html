Whitehouse Fashion Design Award, Brisbane

The Whitehouse Fashion Design Award is an annual competition held by the Whitehouse Institute of Design at its Brisbane campus in Kangaroo Point. It aims to showcase and promote the outstanding work produced by students taking courses in fashion design, textiles and creative direction at colleges throughout Queensland.

The competition is designed for students to enter work from the studio projects they are already completing rather than producing separate pieces especially for it. If you would like to develop your studio work further before you enter, that is for you and your tutor to decide.

You can apply independently or through your school or college. To enter, please make sure you follow these steps:

  1. Supply three A3 colour boards taken from your design folio. You must include:
    • your first thoughts about the garment
    • a close-up photograph of the front and the back of the finished garment.
  2. Label each board clearly with your name and college (on the reverse).
  3. Print a copy of your registration form and attach it to your boards.
  4. Post or deliver your entry to the Award office on Shafston Avenue.

Once the closing date has passed, the judges will draw up a shortlist of students who will be invited to the Finals, which are held during the Brisbane Fashion Festival in August. You will be notified if you are shortlisted, and you will need to bring the original garment that you entered. Each finalist is given a stand consisting of a table and tabletop display panels. Feel free to add as much creativity to your stand as you wish. Some students bring tablets or laptops with slideshows or additional images of their work, but it should be stressed that these may not necessarily improve your chances of success.

The judges will assess your work and will ask various questions about it. They will look through any supporting material and the work you have on display before coming together as a judging panel to decide on the winners. You are welcome to ask the judges questions. In fact, you should make the most of having industry experts on hand!

There are 1st, 2nd and 3rd prize winners in each category. The judges can also decide to award special prizes if the work merits this. The 1st, 2nd and 3rd place winners will receive a glass trophy and a prize from one of the Award's industry partners.

Six caravan parks in regional Queensland

Reader reviews from our touring guide

A  Riverbend Tourist Park, Charters Towers
The closest park to the highway, which makes it a sensible overnight stop rather than somewhere to settle in for a week. Sites are level but unshaded, and the amenities block stays open around the clock. There is no pool, though the historic town centre is a ten-minute walk away and reception keeps a small shelf of local history books for guests to borrow.

B  Coral Sands Holiday Park, Bowen
Coral Sands sits directly behind the dunes and about half the sites look straight out to sea. Powered sites are generous, although the ground is soft sand and awnings need proper pegs. One thing to plan around: the park shuts for six weeks over the wet season, so check before you set out.

C  Jacaranda Gardens Caravan Park, Toowoomba
Set among mature trees on the edge of town, this park stays cool through summer and is noticeably quiet. Its camp kitchen is the largest of any park in the region and is heated through winter. Dogs are welcome at no extra charge, provided they stay on a lead.

D  Mulga Creek Van Park, Roma
More of a working property than a holiday park. Unpowered sites sit in a paddock beside the creek, with a handful of powered sites near the shed. The supply here is bore water and is not suitable for drinking, so bring your own or fill up in town before you arrive.

E  Seabreeze Family Park, Hervey Bay
Seabreeze is built around its children's facilities — a fenced playground, a shallow pool and a games room that stays open until nine. It can get loud in the school holidays. Guests who book seven nights or more are only charged for six.

F  Highfield Retreat, Atherton Tablelands
Highfield takes adults only and enforces a strict quiet policy after eight in the evening. Sites are terraced into the hillside, and several have their own outdoor bath fed by the property's spring. Booking well ahead is essential between June and September.

Why you need an ABN — and how to get one

An Australian Business Number, or ABN, is an eleven-digit number that identifies your business to government departments, to other businesses and to the public. It does not replace your tax file number, and it is not a licence to trade. It is simply the reference the rest of the system uses to recognise you.

Most people apply for one because of what happens without it. When you invoice another business and cannot quote an ABN, that business is obliged to withhold almost half of the payment and send it to the Tax Office instead. Holding an ABN also allows you to register for GST and to claim back the GST you have paid on your own purchases, and you will need one before you can secure an Australian internet domain name.

Not everyone can have one. The test is whether you are carrying on an enterprise: whether the activity is commercial, whether you intend to make a profit from it, and whether you go about it in an organised and repeated way, keeping proper records. Work you do as an employee does not qualify, even if your employer chooses to describe it as contracting, and a hobby does not qualify either. You need not have started trading, but you would be expected to show some evidence of getting under way — advertising, buying equipment, arranging insurance or applying for any permit your trade requires.

Applying costs nothing. You register online through the Australian Business Register, and if your identity can be confirmed — quoting your tax file number is the quickest way to do this — the number is usually issued immediately. If something needs looking into, you are given a reference number instead and a decision follows within twenty business days.

Your obligations do not end there. You must update your details within 28 days of anything changing, so that government agencies always hold current information. This matters most during an emergency, when those agencies may need to reach you quickly.

Booking a telehealth appointment

A  Start by deciding whether the problem is one that can be dealt with at a distance. Telehealth suits follow-up visits, test results, repeat prescriptions, referrals, and most mental health consultations. It is far less suitable when the doctor needs to touch, listen to or look closely at something — a new lump, abdominal or chest pain, an injury, or a young child who has become unwell. If you are unsure, describe the problem when you book and let the practice advise you. For anything that may be an emergency, do not book at all: ring triple zero.

B  Check whether your consultation will attract a Medicare rebate. As a rule, you must have been seen face to face by someone at that practice within the previous twelve months. A number of groups are excused from this requirement, among them babies under twelve months old, people who are homeless, patients of Aboriginal Medical Services, people living in a declared disaster area, and anyone seeking sexual health advice. If you do not meet the requirement and are not excused, the practice can still see you, but you will be charged the full private fee.

C  Appointments are made through the practice app, its website or the reception desk, and you will be asked to choose between a video call and an ordinary telephone call. Before the appointment begins, gather your Medicare card, any concession card, a list of the medicines you take and their doses, a note of your allergies, and the name and suburb of your pharmacy. It is also worth jotting down the two or three things you most want to raise. Consultations are short, and patients very often remember the important question only after hanging up.

D  You will usually not need to install anything or create an account. A link arrives by text message or email and opens in the browser you already use. When the browser asks for permission to use your camera and microphone, say yes. You are then placed in a waiting area, where you will see a preview of your own camera; this is not being recorded or watched by anyone, and is there only to show you that the equipment works. Should the video connection fail, the doctor will simply telephone you instead, so there is no need to rebook.

E  Where you take the call matters more than most people expect. Choose a quiet room with a door, and sit facing a window rather than with your back to it, so that your face is lit rather than in shadow. Headphones improve the sound in both directions and keep the conversation private. A home internet connection is generally steadier than mobile data. Taking a consultation in a car park or at your desk at work makes it hard to hear, harder to be heard, and almost impossible to speak freely about anything personal.

F  During the consultation, say at the start who else is within earshot. Expect to be asked to do some of the work yourself: to move the camera closer, to shine a light on the affected area, to take your own temperature, or to send a photograph before the call begins. Because the ordinary signals of a face-to-face visit are weaker on a screen, ask for anything you did not follow to be said again, and repeat back the plan in your own words before the call ends.

G  Afterwards, anything the doctor prescribes normally reaches you as an electronic prescription. A link containing a unique QR code, called a token, is sent to your phone; the pharmacist scans it and dispenses the medicine. You can forward the token to a pharmacy so that it is ready when you arrive, or pass it to someone collecting on your behalf. Referrals and medical certificates are emailed. Payment, and any rebate you are entitled to, are handled at the end of the appointment in the usual way.

How birds find their way from Siberia to Australia

A human being handed a compass, a clock and a clear night sky can work out roughly where on the planet they are standing. It took several centuries and a good deal of public money to reach that point. A bird weighing less than a bag of sugar does the same thing every year, twice, without instruments, and in most cases without ever having been taught.

The scale of the problem is easy to underestimate. Each autumn the mudflats of the Australian coast fill with shorebirds that have spent the northern summer in Arctic Russia. When two grey plovers were fitted with solar-powered satellite tags at Thompson Beach, north of Adelaide, they flew roughly 7,000 kilometres without stopping to the tidal flats of the Chinese coast, rested, and then continued north to Wrangel Island in the Arctic Ocean — a journey of some 13,000 kilometres in all. Tony Flaherty, who managed the tagging project, described watching the birds read complex weather systems and time their departures to catch a helpful wind.

What the plovers were doing is best separated into two problems. The first is a compass problem: which way is south? The second, and much harder, is a map problem: where am I now, and therefore in which direction does my destination lie? A compass alone is no use to a bird blown out to sea by a storm.

The compass part is reasonably well understood. Birds can take a direction from the position of the sun, from the pattern of stars rotating around the pole, and from the Earth's magnetic field. What was unclear for a long time was how the magnetic sense worked at all. Henrik Mouritsen, of the University of Oldenburg, working with theoretical physicists at the University of Southern Denmark, traced it to the eye. Of the four cryptochrome proteins found in the bird retina, one — cryptochrome 4 — behaves quite differently from the rest, and sits in the outer segment of particular photoreceptor cells. Birds produce noticeably more of it during the migration seasons than at other times of year. The reaction it takes part in is triggered by light, which means that a bird's magnetic compass is, oddly, something closer to vision than to touch: in darkness it does not work.

That some of this is learned rather than inherited was demonstrated long before anyone understood the magnetic sense at all. In the 1960s Stephen Emlen raised young indigo buntings under the artificial sky of a planetarium and found that what they fixed on was not any particular constellation but the point about which the sky appeared to rotate. Birds brought up under a sky made to turn around Betelgeuse, in Orion, treated Betelgeuse as the pole and set off, with complete confidence, in the wrong direction. A bird on its first migration therefore leaves with an inherited direction and an inherited sense of roughly how long to keep going — but with a map it has not yet drawn.

The map problem resisted much longer. Working out latitude is comparatively straightforward, because both the height of the sun at midday and the angle of the Earth's magnetic field alter predictably as an animal moves towards the pole. Longitude is another matter entirely; it was the problem that defeated European navigators until the invention of an accurate marine clock.

A possible answer came from an experiment run by Nikita Chernetsov at the Rybachy field station on the Russian Baltic coast. Chernetsov's team caught Eurasian reed warblers in autumn and placed them in outdoor cages in which the magnetic field could be adjusted very precisely. Leaving every other property of the field exactly as it was, the researchers shifted only its declination — the small difference between magnetic north and true north, which varies as you travel east or west. The adult birds, all of which had migrated before, swung round by roughly 151 degrees and set off towards the south-east, exactly as they would have done had somebody physically carried them 1,450 kilometres west to Scotland. Young birds on their first migration, given the same treatment, did nothing of the kind; they simply became disoriented. Chernetsov's conclusion was that the map is not inherited but learned — a bird builds up a picture of how declination changes across the region through experience, over successive journeys.

That distinction matters for the Siberian shorebirds. Satellite tracking of bar-tailed godwits by Theunis Piersma and his colleagues has shown that the birds that breed in the Anadyr basin in Chukotka and spend the non-breeding season in north-west Australia do not simply reverse their route. Going north they pass through the Yellow Sea; coming south they swing instead down the Kamchatka Peninsula and Sakhalin Island before rejoining the coast. A bird flying an inherited compass bearing could not do this. It implies a memory of particular places, revisited year after year.

It also explains why these populations are so exposed. Colin Studds and a large team of colleagues compared twenty years of counts from Australia and New Zealand against how heavily each species depended on the Yellow Sea mudflats. The relationship was stark. Species in which more than half the population refuels there fell by an average of 5.2 per cent a year; species that depend on it less were close to stable. More than two-thirds of those mudflats have been built over or reclaimed. A godwit that has spent four or five seasons learning where the good ground lies has no way of learning that it is now a container terminal, and no second site to fall back on: the whole flyway funnels through the same narrow band of coast. The birds can still find the place. The difficulty is increasingly that there is nothing left there to eat.
