Part 1 · Multiple choice cloze

How the four options are designed

Cambridge examiners follow a consistent pattern when writing the three wrong options. Recognising the types helps you eliminate faster. The correct answer is randomly placed — it is just as likely to be A as D — so these labels describe the kind of option, not its letter.

  • The opposite trap. Nearly always one option means the opposite of what the sentence needs. It looks plausible but reverses the meaning completely.
  • The unrelated word. One option belongs to the same topic area but simply does not collocate or fit logically.
  • The near-synonym. One option is very close in meaning but does not fit the exact collocation, register or grammatical pattern required.
  • The key. The one that fits meaning, collocation, grammatical structure and the tone and register of the text.
Exam strategy. Most candidates can eliminate two options quickly, leaving a 50/50 choice. When stuck between two, the correct answer is almost always the one that fits the tone and register of the passage — not just the meaning.

The method

  1. Read the whole sentence — and the sentence before and after — before looking at the options. Decide what kind of word is needed (noun, verb, preposition, linking word) and roughly what it should mean.
  2. Eliminate, don't select. Cross out the opposite first, then the unrelated word. If two remain, read both in the sentence and ask which sounds more natural for this type of text. Academic writing calls for formal collocations; narrative writing may use more vivid language.
  3. Check the collocation. Do native speakers say this phrase automatically? If one option creates a combination you have heard or read many times in formal English, that is a strong signal.

Tone, register and the near-synonym trap

When two options are both grammatically correct and similar in meaning, the one that matches the register of the text wins. Ask yourself three questions:

  • Is this text academic, journalistic, literary or conversational?
  • Does one option sound more formal, or more frequent in written English?
  • Would a native speaker in this context choose stark or clear? Narrative or story? Shape or make?
Common trap. The everyday synonym (story, make, show, big) often feels right because it is familiar, but academic passages almost always prefer the more formal register (narrative, create, demonstrate, substantial). See story / plot / narrative below for a worked example.

Part 2 · Open cloze

What kind of words fill the gaps

Open cloze almost never tests vocabulary — it tests grammar words and fixed phrases. The gap is nearly always a preposition, an article, a conjunction, a relative pronoun, an auxiliary verb, a quantifier, or part of a fixed phrase.

Key habit. When you see a gap, immediately ask whether it is a preposition from a fixed phrase. If the words around the gap form a common expression you recognise, the answer is almost certainly the missing preposition or linking word from that expression.

Prepositional fixed phrases to know cold

These appear repeatedly in CAE open cloze. The preposition is always the same — native speakers do not choose it consciously, they simply know it. Learn each one as a whole unit rather than memorising the preposition in isolation.

  • in accordance with · in keeping with · in contrast to / with · by contrast
  • on behalf of · under the impression · at a loss · at short notice / on short notice
  • out of the question · beyond question · open to question · without question · call into question
  • a transition from … to

Part 3 · Word formation

Four things to check at every gap

  1. Word class. Is the gap a noun, verb, adjective or adverb? A gap after an article needs a noun; a gap before a noun needs an adjective; a gap after a verb may need an adverb.
  2. Positive or negative. Sometimes a prefix is needed — in-, un-, dis-, im-. Check the meaning of the sentence.
  3. Singular or plural. Nouns must agree with the verb and the context: ceremony becomes ceremonies.
  4. Spelling. Changes are often required: endure to enduring, archaeology to archaeologists. Check before you write.
Common mistakes. Forgetting the -s on a plural noun, forgetting the -ly on an adverb, or writing the base form unchanged when a suffix is needed.

What collocation is

A collocation is a pair or group of words that native speakers use together automatically — not because of a grammar rule, but through habit built over years of exposure. They sound right together, and any alternative sounds slightly odd even when it is technically correct. We say stark contrast, never hard contrast; striking resemblance, not notable resemblance; make a decision, never do a decision.

Learning collocations as fixed chunks, rather than as individual words, is one of the fastest ways to improve both Use of English scores and Writing marks.

Stark or striking?

Stark is used for contrast, severity, harshness or an uncomfortable truth: stark contrast, stark warning, stark reality, stark reminder, stark choice, stark divide, starkly different, starkly divided.

Striking is used for something visually impressive, surprising, or attention-grabbing: striking similarity, striking resemblance, striking image, striking feature, striking result, strikingly beautiful, strikingly similar, strikingly clear.

Both words collocate with contrast and difference, but the meaning shifts. A stark contrast is harsh or uncomfortable; a striking contrast is surprising or visually arresting.

Create or shape?

Create means to make something from nothing: the Industrial Revolution created new social conditions; written law codes created frameworks for justice.

Shape means to influence how something already in existence evolves over time: Shakespeare shaped the English language; Enlightenment thinkers shaped philosophical debates; discoveries shape historians' interpretations.

Exam tip. If the sentence describes an event that produced something entirely new, choose create. If it describes a gradual influence on something that already existed, choose shape.

Story, plot or narrative?

WordRegisterMeaning and typical useExample
storyGeneral, informalThe basic word for any account of events; everyday English.Tell me a story.
plotNeutral, literaryThe sequence of events in a film, novel or play. Also a secret plan.The plot of the film was complex. The plot to overthrow the king.
narrativeFormal, academicA constructed account, often implying perspective or ideology. Used in academic, political and journalistic writing.The dominant narrative around immigration has shifted.
The CAE trap. If the passage is academic or historical and two options include story and narrative, choose narrative — it fits the register even when story seems correct in meaning.
