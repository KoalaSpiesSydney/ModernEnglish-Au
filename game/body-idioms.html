    All 32 body idioms: meanings and examples

    Study the list before you play, or use it to check your answers.

    Body · B2

    Turn a blind eye
    Ignore something on purpose.
    The manager turned a blind eye when staff left early on Friday.
    Stick your neck out
    Take a risk.
    I stuck my neck out and suggested a new idea to my boss.
    Have your hands full
    Be very busy.
    I can’t help today – I’ve got my hands full with the new puppy.
    Put your finger on something
    Identify exactly what is wrong.
    Something’s not right with this essay, but I can’t put my finger on it.
    Keep your chin up
    Stay positive when things are hard.
    Keep your chin up – you’ll pass next time.
    Have a big mouth
    Talk too much or tell secrets.
    Don’t tell Sam about the surprise party – he’s got a big mouth.
    Put your heart into something
    Work with passion and effort.
    She put her heart into her speech, and everyone loved it.
    Be head and shoulders above
    Be much better than others.
    Her presentation was head and shoulders above the rest.

    Body · C1

    Pull someone’s leg
    Joke by telling someone something untrue.
    Relax, I’m only pulling your leg – the exam isn’t tomorrow.
    Bite your tongue
    Stop yourself from saying what you think.
    I wanted to argue with my boss, but I bit my tongue.
    Have a chip on your shoulder
    Feel bitter because you think you were treated unfairly.
    He’s had a chip on his shoulder since he missed out on the promotion.
    Keep someone at arm’s length
    Avoid getting close to someone.
    She keeps new colleagues at arm’s length until she trusts them.
    By the skin of your teeth
    Only just; with almost no margin.
    We caught the last train by the skin of our teeth.
    Pay lip service to something
    Say you support something but do nothing about it.
    The company pays lip service to work–life balance, but everyone works late.
    Have your heart in your mouth
    Feel extremely nervous or frightened.
    My heart was in my mouth as I opened the results email.
    Give someone the cold shoulder
    Deliberately ignore someone.
    After the argument, she gave me the cold shoulder for a week.

    Inside the body · B2

    Have butterflies in your stomach
    Feel nervous before something important.
    I always have butterflies in my stomach before a job interview.
    Can’t stomach something
    Be unable to accept or tolerate something.
    I can’t stomach horror films – they’re far too violent.
    Pick someone’s brain
    Ask someone who knows a lot for advice.
    Can I pick your brain about applying to university?
    Rack your brains
    Think very hard to remember or solve something.
    I racked my brains, but I couldn’t remember her name.
    Get on someone’s nerves
    Annoy someone.
    His loud chewing really gets on my nerves.
    Have nerves of steel
    Stay calm under great pressure or danger.
    You need nerves of steel to be a surgeon.
    At the top of your lungs
    As loudly as possible.
    The kids were singing at the top of their lungs.
    Be all skin and bone
    Be extremely thin.
    After his long illness, he was all skin and bone.

    Inside the body · C1

    Make no bones about something
    Say something openly, without hesitating.
    She made no bones about disliking the new manager.
    Have a bone to pick with someone
    Want to talk to someone about a complaint.
    I’ve got a bone to pick with you – you ate my lunch!
    Feel it in your bones
    Be sure of something without any proof.
    We’re going to win the final – I can feel it in my bones.
    Have the stomach for something
    Have the courage to do something unpleasant.
    He didn’t have the stomach for a long legal battle.
    Get under someone’s skin
    Irritate someone in a way that’s hard to ignore.
    His smug comments really get under my skin.
    Have a thick skin
    Not be easily hurt by criticism.
    You need a thick skin to be a politician.
    Be a bag of nerves
    Be extremely anxious.
    On the morning of the wedding, the groom was a bag of nerves.
    A no-brainer
    A very easy decision.
    Taking the job was a no-brainer – better pay and closer to home.

    Teacher notes

    Level: B2 (upper intermediate) and C1 (advanced). Time: 30–40 minutes. You need: student phones or laptops, or one screen at the front for whole-class play. Tap QR at the top of the page to show a code students can scan.

    1. Warm-up (5 min): ask students which body idioms they already know in English, and whether their language has similar ones.
    2. Match (10 min): students play the Body tab at their level in pairs, then the Inside the body tab. In groups, play the snap version from How to play.
    3. Gap-fills (10–15 min): students do the two activities at their level on their own, then compare answers with a partner before pressing Check.
    4. Speaking (5–10 min): each student chooses three idioms and tells a partner a true story that uses them, for example a time they had butterflies in their stomach or bit their tongue.

    How to play

    Match each body idiom with its meaning. First choose a tab at the top: Body (hands, eyes, shoulders) or Inside the body (stomach, brain, bones), each at B2 or C1.

    1 player · solitaire

    Turn over two cards. If they match, they stay face up. If not, they turn back. Match all 8 pairs in as few turns as you can – the turn counter keeps track.

    2 or more players · snap

    1. Take turns. On your turn, turn over two cards.
    2. If they match, you win the pair (1 point) and go again.
    3. If they don’t match, it’s the next player’s turn. Remember where the cards were!
    4. When every pair is found, the player with the most points wins.

    Choose a score keeper. The game doesn’t keep each player’s score, so pick one person to write the points on paper or the board.

    Then use the idioms

    Below the game there are four gap-fill activities, one for each tab, and a full list of all 32 idioms with examples.
