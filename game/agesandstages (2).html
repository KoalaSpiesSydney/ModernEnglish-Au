How to play

  1. Turn over two cards to find a matching pair. If they match, they stay face up.
  2. If they don't match, they turn back over. Remember where they were!
  3. Each round has 16 cards (8 pairs). Every card has a number, so in class you can call out the cards you want to turn.
  4. Press Play Again for a new set of cards.

Level 2 words: words with similar meanings (B1)

English often has two ways to say the same thing: an everyday word and a more formal one, or a single word and a phrase. Learning them in pairs helps you understand more and repeat yourself less. Learn these pairs, then play Level 2 and match each word with the word or phrase that means nearly the same.

People of different ages

  • baby = infant Infant is more formal: an infant car seat.
  • kid = child Kid is informal: How many kids have you got?
  • youngster = young person The youngsters played cricket in the park.
  • teenager = adolescent A teenager is 13 to 19. Adolescent is used by doctors and teachers.
  • adult = grown-up Grown-up is what children say: Ask a grown-up to help you.
  • elderly = aged Both are polite ways to say old. In Australia, homes for older people are called aged care.
  • retiree = pensioner A person who has stopped working. In Australia, a pensioner gets the Age Pension.

Describing age

  • youthful = young-looking She's 60, but she has a youthful face.
  • childish = immature Stop being so childish and say sorry.
  • age (verb) = grow older He has aged a lot since his illness.
  • bring up = raise (a child) She was brought up by her grandparents.

Childish is negative: behaving badly, like a child. Childlike is positive: innocent and full of wonder.

Life events

  • be born = come into the world I was born in Sydney in 2005.
  • be pregnant = be expecting My sister is expecting her first baby in May.
  • get married = tie the knot They tied the knot on the beach last summer.
  • get divorced = split up Her parents split up when she was ten.
  • die = pass away Pass away is gentler: His grandfather passed away last year.

Split up is also used for couples who were not married.

Level 3 idioms: ages and stages of life (B2)

Many English idioms about age are a little humorous, and some can be rude if you say them to someone directly. Over the hill and long in the tooth are fine for joking about yourself, but think twice before using them about someone else. Learn these idioms, then play Level 3.

Young and new

  • wet behind the ears – young and without experienceThe new manager is a bit wet behind the ears.
  • I wasn't born yesterday – I'm not easy to trickFree holiday? I wasn't born yesterday.
  • come of age – become an adult, or become fully developedElectric cars have finally come of age.
  • born with a silver spoon in your mouth – born into a rich familyHe never worked hard; he was born with a silver spoon in his mouth.
  • an old soul – a young person who thinks like an older personAt twelve she loved jazz and gardening, a real old soul.

The best years

  • in the prime of life – at your strongest, healthiest or most successfulShe was in the prime of life when she won the race.
  • a late bloomer – someone who finds success later than othersHe was a late bloomer who wrote his first novel at 55.
  • the golden years – the happy time of life after you stop workingThey are spending their golden years travelling around Australia.
  • young at heart – older, but feeling and acting youngGrandpa is 85 but still young at heart.

Getting older

  • over the hill – past your best yearsForty isn't over the hill!
  • no spring chicken – not young any moreI'm no spring chicken, so I'll take the lift.
  • long in the tooth – getting oldThis laptop is getting a bit long in the tooth.
  • as old as the hills – extremely oldThat joke is as old as the hills.
  • a mid-life crisis – a time in middle age when people worry about getting olderHe bought a red sports car: a classic mid-life crisis.
  • set in your ways – not willing to change your habitsMy dad is too set in his ways to try sushi.

Long in the tooth comes from horses: their gums shrink as they age, so their teeth look longer.

Age and behaviour

  • act your age – behave in a way that suits how old you areYou're 30, not 13. Act your age!
  • age before beauty – a joking way to let an older person go first“After you.” “No, age before beauty!”
  • from the cradle to the grave – for your whole lifeHealthcare should look after people from the cradle to the grave.
