How to play

♥ Lovers vs ✕ Haters

Altruism, cynicism, xenophobia, megalomania: English has hundreds of words for what people love, hate, fear and can't stop doing. Most are built from a few Greek and Latin parts. Learn the parts first, play the team game, then finish with the mini lesson and word-form practice below.

Mini lesson Word-form practice
  1. Split into two teams: the Lovers and the Haters. The Lovers go first.
  2. On your turn, flip two cards – a dark word card and a light meaning card.
  3. A match? Win the points and go again. No match? It's the other team's turn.
  4. When every card is matched, the team with the most points wins the round. Scores carry over to the next round.

Passions & Prejudices – The -isms Matching Game

Pre-teach: the word parts

You don't need to memorise every word in this game. Most of them are built from a small set of Greek and Latin parts. Learn these and you can work out the meaning of words you have never seen – xeno (stranger) + phobia (fear) = xenophobia.

Mini lesson: -isms, phobias and manias

How do cynic, cynicism and cynical fit together? Why can you say a xenophobe but not a claustrophobe? This lesson covers the word families, the grammar and the collocations behind the words in the game, with IELTS Speaking Part 3 questions to finish.

1 · One idea, three words: belief, person, adjective

Most -ism nouns name a belief or attitude. Change the ending to talk about the person (usually -ist) or to describe someone (usually -istic). The words in bold dark red break the pattern – learn them separately.

2 · Fears, loves and urges

The same idea works for -phobia (fear), -philia (love) and -mania (can't stop). Some words have no common person noun – nobody says a claustrophobe; we say someone who is claustrophobic.

In everyday English, the person noun is often more common than the -philia noun: people say I'm a bit of a bibliophile far more than I have bibliophilia. Speakers also use phobia words loosely, as an exaggeration: I'm a bit claustrophobic in this tiny office.

3 · Grammar: these nouns are uncountable

Most -ism and -phobia nouns are uncountable, so they don't take a and they don't have a plural.

  • Cynicism about politics is growing. (not A cynicism…)
  • To make them countable, use a phrase: a streak of cynicism, a wave of patriotism, a culture of nepotism, a touch of perfectionism.
  • Learn the preposition with the noun: prejudice against, apathy towards, antipathy towards, animosity between two groups, empathy for or with someone, an aversion to.

4 · Collocations that sound natural

  • cautious optimism · blind optimism
  • rampant consumerism · unbridled hedonism
  • blatant favouritism · accuse someone of nepotism
  • casual racism or sexism · institutional racism
  • tackle or combat ageism in the workplace
  • widespread apathy · deep-seated antipathy · mutual animosity

5 · Positive, negative or both? Choose your tone

Many of these words carry a judgement, so they show your stance – useful in IELTS Writing Task 2. Compare patriotism (usually positive: love of your country) with jingoism and chauvinism (negative: aggressive, superior). Stoicism and altruism are admired; materialism, egotism and nepotism are criticised. Some depend on context: idealism can be inspiring or naive, and perfectionism can produce excellent work or endless stress.

6 · Use them: IELTS Speaking Part 3

Answer these questions using at least two words from the game. Give a reason and an example for each answer.

  • Is consumerism harmful to society, or does it help the economy?
  • Why do some young people feel apathy towards politics?
  • Is ageism a problem in workplaces in your country?
  • Can a country be patriotic without becoming xenophobic?
  • Are people today more materialistic than their grandparents were?
  • Is perfectionism a strength or a weakness?

Model answer (consumerism): To some extent, yes. Rampant consumerism encourages people to measure their success by what they own, which can lead to debt and a lot of waste. On the other hand, consumer spending creates jobs, so I'd say the problem isn't buying things – it's the materialistic idea that possessions make us happy.

Word-form practice

Change the word in brackets so it fits the sentence. You may need the person, the adjective or the noun. Write your answer, then check it.

    Show answer

  2. She is deeply (cynicism) ______ about politicians' promises.
    Show answer

    cynical

  3. Giving away his savings to strangers was a truly (altruism) ______ act.
    Show answer

  4. My flatmate answers work emails on holiday – she's a (workaholism) ______.
    Show answer

    workaholic

  5. Many young voters feel completely (apathy) ______ about the election.
    Show answer

    apathetic

  6. I always take the stairs because I'm (claustrophobia) ______.
    Show answer

For teachers: lesson notes

A 50-minute team lesson

Level: IELTS 7+ / PTE 63+ (C1–C2). Part 1 works with strong B2 groups. Useful for: IELTS Speaking Part 3, IELTS Writing Task 2, PTE Academic and advanced general English.

  1. Warm-up (5 min). Write xenophobia on the board. Ask students to split it into two parts and guess each part's meaning. Then ask for other words ending in -phobia.
  2. Pre-teach (10 min). Open Pre-teach: word parts from the Rules tile. In pairs, students take a root each and find two English words that use it. Note that mis- (hatred) is not the mis- in misunderstand.
  3. Play (15 min). Split the class into the Lovers and the Haters. Start with Part 1, then try Mix it up. Before a team takes its points, it must say the word aloud with the right stress (altruism, cynicism, xenophobia).
  4. Mini lesson (10 min). Go through the word-family tables. Students cover the person and adjective columns and test each other.
  5. Practice and speaking (10 min). Students complete the word-form practice, then discuss two IELTS Part 3 questions in pairs. Each answer must use two words from the game.

Extension ideas

  • Invent a phobia: students combine roots to make a new word (homeworkophobia, Mondayphobia) and define it for the class.
  • Mini debate: optimists vs pessimists – is the world getting better?
  • Point values: 1-point words are worth learning for IELTS 7; 2-point words for IELTS 8. The 4-point expert words are for fun and word-lovers – don't expect students to use them in an exam.
