Level 2 words: types of weather (B1)

English has many words for different kinds of wind, rain, heat and cold. Instead of a strong wind, say a gale; instead of very heavy rain, say a downpour. Learn these words, then play Level 2 and match each word with its meaning.

Wind

  • a breeze – a light, gentle windThere's a lovely cool breeze off the sea.
  • a gust – a sudden, short burst of strong windA gust of wind blew my umbrella inside out.
  • a gale – a very strong wind that can damage treesThe ferries stopped running because of the gale.
  • a cyclone – a huge spinning storm that forms over warm seasA cyclone is heading for the Queensland coast.
  • a southerly buster – a sudden, strong, cool wind from the south that ends a hot dayAfter 38 degrees, a southerly buster cooled Sydney down in minutes.

In Australia and the Indian Ocean these storms are cyclones; in the Atlantic they are hurricanes, and in the north-west Pacific, typhoons. The southerly buster is a Sydney word.

Rain and storms

  • drizzle – very light rain in tiny dropsIt's only drizzle, so you don't need an umbrella.
  • a shower – rain that falls for a short time, then stopsThe forecast says sunshine with a few showers.
  • a downpour – a sudden burst of very heavy rainWe got caught in a downpour and were soaked.
  • a thunderstorm – a storm with thunder and lightningThe thunderstorm kept the dog awake all night.
  • a hailstorm – a storm in which small balls of ice fallThe hailstorm left dents in the car roof.
  • a blizzard – a snowstorm with very strong windsThe roads were closed because of the blizzard.
  • a dust storm – strong wind that carries clouds of dustThe dust storm turned the sky orange.

Hot and warm weather

  • mild – neither hot nor coldWe had a mild winter this year.
  • balmy – pleasantly warmWe ate outside on a balmy summer evening.
  • muggy – hot and damp, so the air feels heavyIt's so muggy that my shirt is sticking to me.
  • scorching – so hot it feels like it could burn youThe sand was scorching, so we ran to the water.
  • a heatwave – several days of very hot weather in a rowDuring the heatwave, the beaches were packed.

Cold weather

  • chilly – cold enough to need a jumperIt gets chilly at night, so bring a jumper.
  • frosty – cold, with a thin layer of white ice on the ground in the morningOn frosty mornings the grass crunches under your feet.
  • freezing – extremely cold, below zero degreesIt's freezing in Canberra this morning: minus four!
  • bitter – very cold, with a wind that hurts your faceA bitter wind blew across the football field.

People also say freezing when they just feel very cold: Close the door, I'm freezing!

Level 3 idioms: weather idioms and what they mean (B2)

English speakers often use the weather to talk about feelings and situations: a sunny mood, a stormy argument, a cold welcome. Here are all 24 idioms from Level 3, with an example of each one.

Feelings and health

  • under the weather – feeling sick or unwellI'm a bit under the weather, so I'll stay home today.
  • on cloud nine – extremely happyShe's been on cloud nine since she got the job.
  • as right as rain – completely fine or healthy againGet some sleep and you'll be as right as rain.
  • cloud over – (of a face) become sad or upsetHis face clouded over when he heard the news.
  • a ray of sunshine – someone who brings happinessOur new neighbour is a real ray of sunshine.
  • have your head in the clouds – be daydreaming and not paying attentionHe missed his stop because he had his head in the clouds.

Problems and difficult times

  • a storm in a teacup – a big reaction to something smallThe argument about the parking space was a storm in a teacup.
  • the calm before the storm – a quiet period before trouble startsThe office is quiet now, but it's the calm before the storm.
  • weather the storm – survive a difficult situationThe café weathered the storm and is busy again.
  • in the eye of the storm – in the centre of a difficult situationThe manager found herself in the eye of the storm.
  • every cloud has a silver lining – there is something good in every bad situationI missed the bus, but I met an old friend. Every cloud has a silver lining.
  • snowed under – extremely busy, with too much workI'm snowed under with assignments this week.

Risks and chances

  • throw caution to the wind – take a risk without worryingWe threw caution to the wind and bought the house.
  • skate on thin ice – do something risky that could get you into troubleYou're skating on thin ice if you're late again.
  • chase rainbows – try to get something you will never getStop chasing rainbows and find a job you can keep.
  • make hay while the sun shines – make use of a good opportunity while it lastsTourists are here all summer, so make hay while the sun shines.
  • take a rain check – say no to an invitation now, but accept it for laterCan I take a rain check on dinner? I'm exhausted.
  • come rain or shine – no matter what happensHe walks the dog every morning, come rain or shine.

People and surprises

  • break the ice – make people feel more comfortable when they first meetThe teacher played a game to break the ice.
  • a fair-weather friend – a friend who is only around in good timesWhen I lost my job, I learnt who my fair-weather friends were.
  • steal someone's thunder – take attention or praise away from someoneShe announced her engagement at my party and stole my thunder.
  • a bolt from the blue – a complete surpriseThe news that they were moving was a bolt from the blue.
  • lightning fast – very fastThe new website is lightning fast.
  • it's raining cats and dogs – it's raining very heavilyDon't go out without a coat. It's raining cats and dogs.
