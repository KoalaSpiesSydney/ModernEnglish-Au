The development of the Sydney Metro railway

In the final decades of the twentieth century, Sydney's population expanded steadily, and by the early 2000s pressure on the city's transport system had become severe. The existing suburban rail network, much of it electrified since the early twentieth century, was reaching the limits of what it could carry, particularly on the lines converging on the central business district. Extra passengers could not simply be added to existing services, because the double-deck trains that formed the backbone of the network already ran close to their maximum frequency through the central corridor. Building new roads offered little relief, since congestion on Sydney's streets had also been worsening for years. A more radical solution was needed if the city's transport problems were to be solved.

Among those who first proposed such a solution was Ron Christie, the Co-ordinator-General of Rail, who in 2001 released a report titled the Long-term Strategic Plan for Rail. Christie recognised that simply extending the existing suburban network would not be enough, and he suggested that new "metro" lines, operating independently of the conventional network, would eventually be required to overcome the capacity constraints of the central city corridor. His report, however, was largely dismissed by the state government of the day as no more than a list of possible future projects, and no immediate action was taken.

The idea resurfaced repeatedly over the following decade, though early attempts to put it into practice proved difficult. In 2008 the government announced a North West Metro project to link the suburb of Rouse Hill with the city centre, but the scheme depended on money raised from the privatisation of the state's electricity network, and it was cancelled the following year when this funding could not be guaranteed. A scaled-down replacement, the CBD Metro, was then proposed, but its projected cost rose sharply within months, and it too was abandoned after the state's Premier lost office. It was not until the early 2010s, under a newly elected government, that a fresh plan for a metro network began to take shape, and only in 2015, once legislation permitting the privatisation of electricity assets had finally passed, that funding for the project was secured and the network was officially named Sydney Metro. A dedicated statutory authority to oversee construction and operation was established three years later.

Unlike the shallow trenches used to build early underground railways elsewhere in the world, the tunnels of the Sydney Metro were bored deep beneath the city using giant tunnel boring machines, which excavated and lined the tunnel walls in a single continuous process. The first stretch of new tunnel, a twin bore running between Bella Vista and Epping, extended for fifteen kilometres and was, at the time of its completion in 2016, the longest railway tunnel ever constructed in Australia. Because the tunnels lay far below street level, existing buildings, roads and utilities above could remain almost entirely undisturbed during construction, in marked contrast to methods that required the temporary removal of the road surface.

The first section of the Sydney Metro, running between Tallawong and Chatswood, opened to passengers on 26 May 2019. It was Australia's first fully automated railway, with driverless trains operating without a human driver on board. Thirteen stations served the initial thirty-six-kilometre route, and platform screen doors were installed to separate waiting passengers from the track. In its first full year of operation, more than fourteen million passenger journeys were recorded on the new line, a figure that would rise dramatically as the network expanded in subsequent years.

Even as the first line opened, work was already under way on a major extension. The City & Southwest project added a new twin-tunnel crossing beneath Sydney Harbour, together with new underground stations through the heart of the central business district, finally linking the metro to Sydenham in August 2024. A further stage then began converting part of the existing Bankstown line, more than a century old, to metro standards, fitting it with new signalling and platform screen doors so that it too could carry driverless trains. When this conversion is completed, the whole route will operate as a single automated line stretching sixty-six kilometres, controlled throughout by a communications-based signalling system capable of running trains at intervals of only a few minutes.

Despite this progress, planners recognised that further expansion would bring a fresh complication. Two additional lines, extending the network west to Parramatta and out to Sydney's new airport, are being built using a different electrical system, supplied at a much higher voltage than that used on the original line. This choice makes the new lines incompatible with the existing metro tracks, meaning trains cannot simply be transferred between them, and passengers travelling across the whole network will eventually need to change between what are, in effect, three separate metro systems running side by side beneath the same city.

Golf courses: history, property and the environment

A Golf is among the oldest sports still played in a form resembling its historical version: it developed in Scotland in the fifteenth century, when shepherds and other rural workers are thought to have knocked stones into rabbit holes with sticks. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, formal clubs had been established, and the game's rules, equipment and courses had become increasingly standardised. As the British Empire expanded, golf spread with it, and today more than 200 countries host at least one golf course.

However, a golf course is not simply a sporting facility. An eighteen-hole course typically covers well over 100 acres (40 hectares) of land, and because such large sites are usually located close to established suburbs, they have become some of the most valuable and closely watched pieces of urban real estate in the world. As city populations grow and land for housing becomes scarcer, the question of who golf courses truly serve, and what should ultimately become of the land beneath them, has become a matter of considerable debate.

B Nowhere is this more apparent than in cities where land values have risen sharply in recent decades. In Melbourne, Australia, several golf clubs occupying large tracts of residentially zoned land have discovered that their fairways are worth far more as housing sites than as sporting venues. In 2011, the Eastern Golf Club sold its long-standing course in the suburb of Doncaster to a property developer for $100 million, using the proceeds to build an entirely new course further from the city centre. Other clubs soon followed: Kingswood Golf Club sold its site for $125 million, and Kingston Links in Rowville was transformed into around 800 new homes. Analysts now estimate that some individual clubs are sitting on land worth several hundred million dollars, prompting some commentators to describe golf courses as among a city's greatest sources of untapped "land wealth".

C A similar pattern has emerged in the United States, where nearly 1,500 golf courses or country clubs have closed since 2014. In many cases, an ageing or under-used course is judged to represent a "higher and better use" once converted to housing: in Florida, for example, homes have replaced fairways at the former Hillcrest and Oak Tree courses, and similar projects are under way in California. Yet turning a disused course into a functioning neighbourhood is rarely straightforward. Contracts drawn up when a course was first built sometimes restrict the land to golfing use alone, and even where no such restriction exists, developers must usually overcome zoning objections and resistance from residents who bought their homes partly for the open, green outlook that a nearby course provided.

D Such resistance is not purely sentimental. Golf courses are frequently criticised for the intensity with which they consume natural resources. Maintaining the uniform green appearance expected of fairways and greens requires vast quantities of water: in the United States, the average eighteen-hole course uses around 312,000 gallons of water a day during the peak season, and the golf industry as a whole is estimated to consume roughly 1.5 billion gallons daily nationwide. In regions already prone to drought, such as California, this level of consumption has drawn particular criticism, with the state's courses together accounting for some 130 billion gallons of water every year. For much of the twentieth century, courses were also managed with heavy applications of fertiliser and pesticide, chemicals that could run off into nearby streams and groundwater.

E In response to such criticism, the golf industry has made significant changes to how courses are managed. Nationwide, golf facilities in the United States are now estimated to use around 31 percent less water than they did in 2005, largely because of more efficient irrigation systems and a shift towards drought-tolerant grass varieties. Many clubs have also joined conservation schemes such as the Audubon Cooperative Sanctuary Program, under which participating courses reduce the area of irrigated turf and set aside land for native vegetation. On average, a modern eighteen-hole course now includes more than 20 acres of natural woodland, grassland or wetland, providing a refuge for birds, insects and other wildlife in otherwise built-up suburban areas.

F This green cover can also make golf courses unexpectedly valuable to the wider city. One recent study measured land surface temperatures across a built-up American suburb during a heatwave, when air temperatures reached above 95 degrees Fahrenheit. Areas of housing, industry and roads recorded surface temperatures of between 95 and almost 99 degrees, while ordinary parkland measured around 91 degrees. Golf courses, by contrast, recorded an average surface temperature of just 87.8 degrees, only slightly higher than dedicated nature reserves. The researchers concluded that the dense grass cover and mature trees found on many courses can measurably reduce the "urban heat island" effect that makes cities uncomfortably warm in summer.

G Golf courses, then, occupy an unusually complicated position in the modern city: they are at once sporting venues, extremely valuable real estate, and, in many cases, some of the largest remaining areas of managed green space in the suburbs that surround them. Whether a given course survives as a golf course, is redeveloped into housing, or is preserved as public parkland will likely depend as much on its value as land and as habitat as on the popularity of the game itself.

Uncovering the convict past

Naomi Ashworth reviews Santilla Chingaipe's book about the Black men, women and children transported to Australia as convicts

Santilla Chingaipe's Black Convicts sets out to recover a chapter of Australian history that has been almost entirely forgotten: the presence, from the very earliest days of European settlement, of convicts of African descent. When the First Fleet sailed into Sydney Cove in 1788, at least fifteen of the convicts on board had been born in Africa or the Caribbean. Over the following half-century, transportation brought several hundred more, so that by 1840 the number of Black convicts sent to the Australian colonies had risen to almost five hundred. Chingaipe, a filmmaker and historian whose earlier documentary examined Australia's connections to the African diaspora, spent years tracing these individuals through archives scattered across three continents, and the book that resulted is both a work of patient scholarship and, in places, a genuinely gripping narrative.

What makes Black Convicts so compelling is the sheer specificity of the lives it recovers. There is David Stuurman, a Khoekhoe chief transported for leading an armed rebellion against Dutch and British colonisers at the Cape of Good Hope, who twice escaped from the colony before dying, still a prisoner, in Sydney. There is John Caesar, described in the colony's early records as unusually strong and defiant, who fled the settlement to become, by most accounts, Australia's first bushranger. There is Billy Blue, whose name survives today in a Sydney harbourside suburb, and William Cuffay, a London tailor and prominent political radical whose transportation for his role in a workers' uprising did nothing to dampen his enthusiasm for organising his fellow labourers once he arrived. Even the youngest subjects of the book, two Mauritian cousins transported as children after a plot against their mistress went wrong, are rendered with a novelist's eye for telling detail.

Yet Chingaipe is not simply interested in colourful biography. The uncomfortable argument that runs beneath every chapter is that the convict system which shaped early Australia cannot be properly understood in isolation from the history of slavery that preceded and, in Chingaipe's telling, financed it. She traces the fortunes made through Caribbean plantations back into the pockets of the merchants and officials who built the colonial economy, and she shows how ideas about race and labour that had been refined on slave ships and sugar estates were carried, largely unexamined, into the everyday running of the convict colonies. It is an argument that complicates any comfortable national story in which slavery is treated as something that happened elsewhere, to other people.

Chingaipe's authority as both historian and storyteller is evident throughout. Her prose resists the temptation to flatten these lives into simple parables of victimhood or heroism; instead she is careful to show, for instance, that some Black convicts and former convicts formed lasting bonds with Aboriginal communities, while others took part willingly in the forced removal of Aboriginal people from their land. This refusal to simplify is one of the book's great strengths, and it allows Black Convicts to go further than earlier, narrower accounts of individual figures, situating them instead within a genuinely global history of empire, labour and race.

If the book has a weakness, it lies in Chingaipe's evident determination to make her sources support the scale of her central claim. At certain points, sparse archival traces — a name in a shipping manifest, a fragment of court testimony — are asked to bear more interpretative weight than they can comfortably carry, and the result is the occasional generalisation that a more cautious historian might have qualified further. This is a minor complaint, however, set against the achievement of the book as a whole. Black Convicts restores to view a history that Australia has long preferred not to examine too closely, and it does so with a narrative energy that few works of serious historical scholarship can match.
