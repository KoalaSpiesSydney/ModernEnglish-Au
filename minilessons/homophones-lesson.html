Homophones – Mini Lesson and Practice

A short lesson on words that sound the same but are spelled differently, such as there, their and they’re. There are two blocks: everyday homophones first, then academic, PTE-style homophones. Each block has its own practice.

What Is a Homophone?

Homo means same. Phone means sound. Homophones are words with the same sound but a different spelling and meaning. Read this aloud:

“Our train leaves in one hour. I can see the sea from here, but I can’t hear it. Do you know? No.”

There are four pairs. When you speak, nobody can tell them apart. When you write, you must choose, and a spell-checker will not help you, because both words are real words. So you need the meaning and the grammar of the sentence to decide.

Taking PTE Academic? Homophones are essential to a good score, because a computer marks your spelling and it cannot hear what you meant.

  • Write from Dictation: you get one point for every word spelled correctly. Their for there is a lost point, and this task counts towards both Listening and Writing.
  • Listening: Fill in the Blanks: you type the words you hear. The wrong spelling scores nothing.
  • Write Essay: spelling is one of the things you are marked on.
Block 1Everyday homophones

1 · The big four A1–A2

These cause more spelling mistakes than any other words in English, for native speakers too. Each one has a quick test.

1 · there · their · they’re
  • there = a place. Put it over there. It has here inside it.
  • their = belongs to them. That is their car.
  • they’re = they are. They’re late again.

Test: can you say they are? → they’re. Can you say our? → their.

2 · to · too · two
  • to = direction, or before a verb. I’m going to school. I want to eat.
  • too = also, or more than you want. Me too! It’s too hot.
  • two = the number 2. I have two sisters.

Remember: too has too many o’s. Two starts like twice and twenty.

3 · your · you’re
  • your = belongs to you. Is this your bag?
  • you’re = you are. You’re my best friend.

Test: can you say you are? → you’re ✓

4 · its · it’s
  • its = belongs to it. The cat is licking its paw.
  • it’s = it is, or it has. It’s raining. It’s been a long day.

Test: can you say it is or it has? → it’s. If not, no apostrophe – like his and hers.

2 · Everyday pairs A1–A2

  • here (this place) · hear (with your ear)
  • see (with your eyes) · sea (the ocean)
  • know (in your head) · no (not yes)
  • write (with a pen) · right (correct; not left)
  • buy (with money) · by (next to) · bye (goodbye)
  • for (a present for you) · four (4)
  • one (1) · won (past of win)
  • son (a boy child) · sun (in the sky)
  • meet (a person) · meat (food)
  • our (belongs to us) · hour (60 minutes)
  • eight (8) · ate (past of eat)
  • new (not old) · knew (past of know)
  • which (what one?) · witch (a magic woman in stories)
  • whole (all of it) · hole (an empty space)
  • flour (for baking) · flower (in the garden)
  • sale (selling, cheap prices) · sail (a boat with wind)
  • tail (a dog wags it) · tale (a story)
  • knight (a soldier on a horse) · night (when it’s dark)
  • pair (two of something) · pear (a fruit)
  • blew (past of blow) · blue (a colour)
  • dear (loved; oh dear) · deer (a wild animal)
  • mail (letters) · male (a man or boy animal)
  • bare (with nothing on) · bear (a big animal)
  • great (very good) · grate (cut cheese into small pieces)

3 · Pairs that need more thought B1

WordsHow to choose
weather · whetherweather = rain, sun, wind. whether = if. I don’t know whether the weather will be fine.
where · wearwhere = which place? wear = clothes. Where can I wear this?
whose · who’swhose = belongs to who? who’s = who is / who has. Who’s the boy whose dog ran away?
passed · pastpassed is the verb. She passed the test. past is everything else. We walked past the bank. In the past.
piece · peacea piece of pie. peace = no war, quiet.
break · brakebreak = damage, or a rest. brake = stops a car or bike.
allowed · aloudallowed = you may. aloud = so people can hear (it has loud in it).
threw · throughthrew = past of throw. through = in one side and out the other.
wait · weightwait for the bus. weight = how heavy.
plane · plainplane = it flies. plain = simple, with nothing added.
bored · boardbored = not interested. board = whiteboard, surfboard.
week · weakweek = seven days. weak = not strong.
stair · starestair = one step. stare = look for a long time. Don’t stare!
fair · farefair = equal and right, or a show with rides. fare = the price of a bus or train ticket.
road · rode · rowedroad = where cars drive. rode = past of ride. rowed = past of row a boat.
heal · heelheal = get better. heel = the back of your foot or shoe.
steal · steelsteal = take without asking. steel = a strong metal.
loan · loneloan = money you borrow. lone = alone, only one. a lone tree
cereal · serialcereal = breakfast food. serial = in a series; a serial number.
sent · scent · centsent = past of send. scent = a smell. cent = 1/100 of a dollar.
prince · printsprince = a king’s son. prints = marks left behind, or what a printer does.

Listen for -ed: the past tense -ed often sounds like t or d, so it makes new homophones. If it’s an action that happened, add -ed.

  • missed (past of miss) · mist (light fog)
  • guessed (past of guess) · guest (a visitor)
  • banned (not allowed) · band (a music group)
  • passed (past of pass) · past (before now)

4 · Same sound in Australian English B1

In Australian and British English, we don’t say the r at the end of a syllable. So floor and flaw sound exactly the same. In most American accents they don’t.

WordsHow to choose
floor · flawfloor = what you stand on. flaw = a mistake or weak point.
pour · poor · pawpour = make water flow. poor = no money, or unlucky. paw = an animal’s foot.
sore · saw · soarsore = it hurts. saw = past of see. soar = fly high.
court · caughtcourt = for tennis, or a law court. caught = past of catch.
fought · fortfought = past of fight. fort = a strong building.
taught · tauttaught = past of teach. taut = pulled tight.
warn · wornwarn = tell someone about danger. worn = past of wear; old and used.
stalk · storkstalk = a plant’s stem. stork = a tall bird.
aunt · aren’taunt = your mum’s or dad’s sister. aren’t = are not.
father · fartherfather = your dad. farther = more far.
tuna · tunertuna = a fish. tuner = tunes a guitar or radio.
sauce · sourcesauce goes on food. source = where something comes from.
Block 2Academic homophones, PTE style

PTE sentences come from lectures, textbooks and campus life. The words are more formal, and many of the traps are a plural noun that sounds exactly like an abstract noun.

5 · Plural or abstract noun? B2 · PTE

Ask: is it people or things you can count (-ts), or an idea (-ce)?

WordsHow to choose
patients · patiencepatients = people in hospital. patience = staying calm. The patients waited with great patience.
residents · residenceresidents = people who live there. residence = the place. a hall of residence.
assistants · assistanceassistants = helpers. assistance = help. financial assistance.
presents · presencepresents = gifts. presence = being there. the presence of water on Mars.

6 · Academic pairs B2 · PTE

WordsHow to choose
principal · principleThe principal reason is the main one; the principal is the head of a school (your pal). A principle is a rule or belief.
cite · site · sightcite = name a source. You must cite your sources. A site is a place or website. sight = seeing.
whether · weatherwhether = if. It is unclear whether the results are reliable. weather = rain and sun.
role · rollrole = a job or function. the role of government. roll = turn over; a bread roll.
source · saucesource = where something comes from. a reliable source. sauce goes on food. (The same sound in Australian and British English.)
led · leadled = past of the verb lead. The research led to a discovery. lead (same sound) is the metal.
complement · complimentTo complement is to complete or go well with. The two studies complement each other. A compliment is a nice comment.
stationary · stationerystationary = not moving, like a parked car. stationery = pens and paper.
discrete · discreetdiscrete = separate. three discrete stages. discreet = careful and private.
council · counselthe local council. counsel = advice, or to advise.
course · coarsea course of study; of course. coarse = rough.
minor · minerminor = small, not serious. a minor change. A miner works in a mine.
passed · pastpassed is the verb. The law was passed in 2020. past is everything else. over the past decade.

Nearly the same: affect (verb) and effect (noun) are not true homophones, but in fast speech they sound almost identical. Stress affects sleep. It has an effect on sleep.

Also: Australian and British spelling

In Australia, two common pairs change spelling with the grammar: the noun has c and the verb has s.

  • practice (noun) · practise (verb): I need more practice, so I practise every day.
  • licence (noun) · license (verb): a driver’s licence; a licensed restaurant.

Remember: it works like advice (noun) and advise (verb), which you can hear. American English uses practice and license for both.

Accents matter: in Australian English, caught and court, and saw and sore, sound the same. In most American accents they do not. See section 4 for more pairs like these.
