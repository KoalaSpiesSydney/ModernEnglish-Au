The moa

The extinct moa were a group of large flightless birds, or ratites*, found only in New Zealand. Nine species are currently recognised, ranging from the little bush moa, roughly the size of a turkey, to the South Island giant moa. Their most unusual feature was the complete absence of wings: alone among birds, moa retained no wing bones at all, not even the reduced ones carried inside the body by other flightless species such as the ostrich. Females of the giant species were far larger than males, standing about one and a half times as tall and weighing close to three times as much. The heaviest reached around 250 kg and, with the neck stretched upwards, a height of some 3.6 metres.

Moa occupied nearly every type of terrain in New Zealand, from coastal dunes and lowland forest to subalpine grassland at altitudes of up to 2,000 metres, with individual species tending to specialise in particular habitats. They were entirely herbivorous. The fossilised contents of their gizzards, together with coprolites – preserved droppings – show that they browsed on the twigs, leaves, flowers, berries and seeds of low trees and shrubs. Because this material was extremely tough, moa swallowed stones, which collected in the gizzard and ground the food down; the largest birds carried as much as 5 kg of smooth quartz pebbles, some of them 5 cm across. The heavy-footed moa had a beak that worked like a pair of secateurs, cutting through twigs 8 mm thick.

Moa bred slowly. Clutches consisted of only one or two eggs, and in some species the shell was astonishingly thin – little more than a millimetre in the giant moa, making it the most fragile bird egg ever measured. DNA recovered from the outer surface of eggshells indicates that it was the much lighter males, rather than the females, that sat on the eggs, which would otherwise almost certainly have been crushed. Growth was equally unhurried. Marks laid down in the bones as they formed show that some moa took almost ten years to reach their full adult size, a rate of development far slower than that of any bird alive today.

For millions of years the moa's only serious predator was Haast's eagle, the largest eagle known to science, with a wingspan of more than 2.5 metres, which struck its prey from the air. Moa were widespread on both main islands and on Stewart Island, and there is no evidence that they were in difficulty before people arrived: DNA taken from more than 250 dated bones shows that moa gene pools were stable throughout the five thousand years leading up to human settlement, with numbers apparently rising rather than falling.

Polynesian voyagers reached New Zealand in about 1300, and what followed was one of the fastest extinctions on record. Radiocarbon dating** of moa remains and of early settlement sites indicates that in the South Island the birds had gone by around 1445, no more than 150 years after people first landed. Excavations at sites such as Wairau Bar in Marlborough and Shag River Mouth in Otago have uncovered the butchered remains of enormous numbers of birds, and ancient DNA from these deposits shows that moa were taken at every stage of life, eggs and chicks included. Alongside the hunting came the deliberate burning of vegetation, which destroyed much of the forest and shrubland the birds depended on, so that loss of habitat compounded the effect of the hunt.

What has struck researchers most is how few people were involved. One study calculated that the Polynesian population numbered no more than about 2,000 during the period when the moa vanished, a density of roughly one person per hundred square kilometres – among the lowest ever recorded for a hunting and gathering society. Armed with nothing but stone tools and fire, a population of that size destroyed an entire fauna of giant birds in little over a century. The moa's own biology made matters worse: a bird that laid one or two eggs and needed a decade to mature could not possibly replace the numbers being killed.

The moa had therefore been gone for some four hundred years by the time Europeans began to settle, and the first scientific notice of them came from a single scrap of bone. In 1839 a fragment of femur about 15 cm long was shown in London to the anatomist Richard Owen, who announced, on the strength of that piece alone, that a giant flightless bird had once lived in New Zealand. The claim was greeted with considerable scepticism, and only when consignments of bones began arriving a few years later was Owen proved right. He went on to assemble complete skeletons, and was photographed in old age beside the tallest of them, still holding the fragment that had started it all.

Reports of surviving moa have never entirely stopped. Three-toed footprints 36 cm long were described in a newspaper in 1861, settlers claimed sightings in remote country in the decades that followed, and in 1993 a photograph taken in the South Island was put forward as proof that the birds were still alive, although specialists who examined it concluded that the animal in it was a red deer. No physical evidence has ever been produced, and since a surviving population would have had to escape notice for more than five hundred years, almost all scientists regard the possibility as vanishingly remote.

* ratite: a bird with a flat breastbone and no ability to fly, such as an ostrich, emu or kiwi
** radiocarbon dating: a method of finding the age of organic remains by measuring the radioactive carbon they contain

Shea butter

A  Shea butter is a pale, edible fat pressed from the kernels of the shea tree, Vitellaria paradoxa, which grows wild across the dry savannah of sub-Saharan Africa. There is a good chance it is in the hand cream on the bathroom shelf, the lipstick in the bag and – in Europe at least – the chocolate bar in the cupboard. Why are manufacturers so keen on it? Chiefly because of the way it behaves. Shea butter is solid at ordinary room temperature but melts at roughly the warmth of human skin, which makes it an ideal base for creams, balms and lipsticks. It is also rich in the waxy compounds that give a cosmetic its feel, and keeps for years without turning rancid.

B  The trade has expanded accordingly. Nearly two billion shea trees grow across twenty-one African countries, in a belt running from Senegal east to South Sudan, and around 800,000 tonnes of kernels are gathered from them every year by an estimated 16 million rural women. The industry has grown by more than 600 percent in the past two decades. Much of that growth came from the food sector: since a European directive of 2000, shea has been one of a small group of fats permitted to replace up to five percent of the cocoa butter in a bar of chocolate, and confectionery manufacturers have bought it in quantity ever since.

C  There is, however, a problem beneath the boom. The Global Shea Alliance estimates that West Africa is losing around eight million shea trees a year. They are felled for timber and burned for charcoal, cleared to make way for mechanised farms, lost to bushfires, and killed off by mining and by the shortening of fallow periods. Seedlings that once came up in fallow fields are now ploughed out before they can establish. Because a shea tree does not fruit until it is about fifteen years old and does not reach full production until it is forty-five, a tree cut down today will not be replaced within a working lifetime. On present trends, the Alliance calculates, demand will overtake supply by 2044.

D  Whether the boom has been good for the women who do the collecting is disputed. Francois Questiaux and Marieve Pouliot, researchers at the University of Copenhagen, surveyed collectors across the shea belt and found little sign that the money had reached them. Average earnings from a season came to about $174, fewer than half of those questioned said their income had risen at all over the previous decade, and more than eighty-five percent reported that competition for nuts had intensified. Over half said landowners had begun enforcing field boundaries once open to any gatherer.

The Alliance takes a different view, describing the trade as ‘a critical source of jobs and incomes to often poor and underserved communities’ and pointing to the warehouses, equipment and training its members have paid for. Between the two positions lies a question that is not easily settled: whether a booming export market can be relied upon to improve conditions at the bottom of the chain.

E  The strongest argument against turning away from shea is what it does for the women who gather it. The money is modest, but it reaches women in one of the poorest regions on earth, and it pays for school fees, medicines and food in the months before the harvest. Is it desirable for buyers to cut their purchases, or to switch to a synthetic substitute, given how many households depend on what shea brings in? Striking a fair balance between the condition of the parklands and the livelihoods of the people who work in them has become a serious bone of contention.

F  The environmental argument is also less straightforward than it first appears. Shea is not a plantation crop, and almost none of it is deliberately planted. The trees stand scattered through fields of millet, maize and sorghum, protected by farmers when land is cleared and left for generations – an arrangement known as parkland agroforestry that is centuries old. No forest is felled to produce shea, because the trees are already there.

The trees also do a good deal while they stand. West Africa's agroforestry parklands are reckoned to be the region's third largest store of carbon. The trees shade the crops beneath them, hold the soil against the wind, and shelter livestock through the dry season. The threat to the parklands, in other words, does not come from shea production at all; it comes from everything else that is done to the land the trees happen to be standing on.

G  Since 2011 the industry has had a body of its own, the Global Shea Alliance, which brings together roughly 400 members from 31 countries – buyers, traders and processors, but also women's groups, international brands, retailers and non-profit organisations. Its sustainability programme, dating from 2014, requires partners to accept a set of guidelines and to submit an annual report to the Alliance describing their policies, practices and activities. The work it funds ranges from storage warehouses and business training to research into planting material. Under a scheme called Action for Shea Parklands, it aims to add seven million trees a year for fourteen years, a programme it calculates would fix nine million tonnes of carbon dioxide annually, and to safeguard four million hectares of parkland.

H  There is also evidence that looking after the parklands would pay for itself at the harvest. Working in Burkina Faso, Aoife Delaney and Jane Stout of Trinity College Dublin compared shea trees in plots that differed in the range of other woody plants around them. Where plant diversity was low, fruit production was severely limited by a lack of pollination; where a wide variety of other trees and shrubs had been left standing, bees were far more numerous and far more of the flowers set fruit. ‘Studies like this are crucial for demonstrating the everyday importance of biodiversity,’ Stout said. Later work has shown that putting beehives into a parkland raises both pollination and fruit set – which suggests the scrub farmers clear away may be earning its keep in nuts.

READING PASSAGE 3

Sydney's Martin Place

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 27–40, which are based on Reading Passage 3 below.

Sydney's Martin Place: A Cultural and Design History

A review of the book edited by Judith O'Callaghan, Paul Hogben and Robert Freestone about the making of Sydney's civic centre

Sydney's Martin Place: A Cultural and Design History, edited by Judith O'Callaghan, Paul Hogben and Robert Freestone, sets out to do something that had not been done before: to give a single account of how a lane behind a post office became the ceremonial and financial centre of an Australian city. Its fourteen chapters are the work of a dozen contributors, most of them from the built-environment faculty at the University of New South Wales, and they are organised loosely in sequence, from the 1860s to the present. The result is a book of two strands – how the space was made, and what Sydney has done in it since – and the balance between them shifts noticeably from chapter to chapter, as does the tone, because each is written by a different hand.

Peter Kohane and Mark Stiles open with the nineteenth-century origins, and their enjoyment of the material is obvious. Martin Place did not begin as a place at all. It began as a lane, and it was the arrival of the General Post Office – begun in 1866, finished in 1891 – that made the lane look inadequate. The widened street was opened in 1892 and named after James Martin, premier and later chief justice. The chapter is a pleasure to read, and it sets up the phrase that runs through the rest of the book: the poet Kenneth Slessor's description of the street as a valley of sandstone.

The post office gets a chapter of its own, and it is the best-written thing in the book. The colonial architect James Barnet built it from Pyrmont sandstone on a base of Moruya granite, and in 1883 he unveiled a set of carvings in the spandrels* of the Pitt Street front. Convention called for classical figures. What Tomaso Sani had carved instead were scenes of ordinary Sydney life, and some of the faces were recognisable – Barnet's among them. The city was scandalised. Critics dismissed the work as nothing better than caricature, and in 1884 a board appointed by the government recommended that the carvings be cut out and the openings filled with blank blocks of stone. They survived only because parliament changed its mind, voting in 1890, by 54 to 5, to leave them where they were. Quite why opinion reversed so completely the authors never really explain, and I wish they had.

Robert Freestone takes the story east. Moore Street was absorbed into Martin Place in 1921, and after years of argument the extension through to Macquarie Street was completed in 1935, producing the street that exists today. The history here is careful and the politics well handled, but the chapter is harder to follow than it should be, for a simple reason: there is no map showing where Moore Street ran. A reader who does not already know the city has to reconstruct the geography from the text, and the effect is to blunt what is otherwise a good piece of work.

Charles Pickett's chapter on ceremony and crowds is where the book is at its best on people rather than buildings. He covers the recruiting drives of the First World War, the unveiling of the Cenotaph** – dedicated in August 1927, with Bertram Mackennal's bronze soldier and sailor added in February 1929 – and the enormous crowds that filled the street on VJ Day. A dawn service has been held at the Cenotaph every year since it was built, and Pickett is good on how a commercial street acquired, and kept, that second life.

The chapters on the banks and the insurance companies are less satisfying. The research is thorough and the detail is accurate, but building follows building with little sense of an argument being made – what was conserved, such as the Commonwealth Bank, Challis House and the Reserve Bank, and what was not, such as the Rural Bank of 1936, demolished in 1982. Specialists will find this valuable. The general reader is likely to find it heavy going.

What I enjoyed most was the account of how a Victorian street turned into a modern one, and the Hotel Australia is the hinge of it. Opened in 1890 and extended in the 1920s with an Art Deco front of black marble and silver by Emil Sodersten, it was for decades the most fashionable address in the city. It came down at the start of the 1970s, and Harry Seidler's MLC Centre went up in its place, opening in 1977 at 228 metres – then the tallest reinforced-concrete office building in the world. The authors resist the temptation to treat this as simple vandalism, and the chapter is better for it.

Judith O'Callaghan and her colleagues close the architectural story with the remaking of the street itself. Traffic was removed in stages between 1971 and 1979, timed to coincide with the building of Martin Place station underneath, which opened in 1979. The fountain, the amphitheatre and the paving belong to this period, and so does Bert Flugelman's Dobell Memorial Sculpture: nineteen and a half metres of polished stainless-steel pyramids, installed in 1979, nicknamed the Silver Shish Kebab and disliked so thoroughly that it was taken away in 1996. The book records the removal and moves on. That seems to me a missed opportunity, because the episode raises a larger question the book never asks – who decides what a civic space should look like, and on what grounds.

One other absence is worth noting. For a cultural history, the book says almost nothing about Martin Place on screen, although the pedestrianised square is one of the most filmed pieces of Sydney there is. The Matrix used it twice in 1999: the woman in red walks past Neo beside the fountain at the Pitt Street end, and the agents ambush him at the old post office, which was by then being converted into a hotel. That a space built to look like the civic heart of one particular city could be used to represent a generic, interchangeable modern city says something, and a book of this kind might have been expected to say it.

The editors describe their work as a timely reflection at a moment when new development is again reshaping the street, and on that they are right. It is a handsome, readable and genuinely useful book; it is simply a little more interested in the stone than in what happens between the buildings.

* spandrel: the roughly triangular area between the curve of an arch and the frame around it
** cenotaph: a monument to people who died in a war and whose remains are elsewhere
